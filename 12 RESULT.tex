This section reports the empirical results. Following the structure of Sections~\ref{sec:pairsbaseline}--\ref{sec:regimemodels}, we first inspect the data and pair-level diagnostics, then back-test the baseline rule, then add the regime-aware extensions. All numbers are net of the half-spread-plus-commission cost model of Section~\ref{sec:walkforward} \parencite{aldridge2013hft,lopezdeprado2018advances}. For each of the three pair constructions we report three monthly episodes side by side---\emph{August 2024}, \emph{December 2024}, and \emph{April 2025}---to keep cross-episode robustness visible at all times \parencite{lopezdeprado2018advances}.

\subsection{Data and pair diagnostics}\label{sec:result-pairdiag}

The three pairs differ substantially in liquidity and per-leg round-trip cost \parencite{roll1984spread,hasbrouck2007microstructure}: GBP/USD--EUR/USD is the cheapest, AUD/USD--NZD/USD is moderately liquid, and EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK is materially more expensive. All six leg-level return series are strongly non-Gaussian, with high kurtosis and significant Ljung--Box statistics on squared returns, consistent with conditional heteroskedasticity \parencite{engle1982arch,bollerslev1986garch,cont2001empirical}. Rolling cointegration tests \parencite{engle1987cointegration,dickey1979distribution,said1984testing} reject the no-cointegration null intermittently for AUD/USD--NZD/USD and GBP/USD--EUR/USD; EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK rejects globally but with a long implied half-life, which makes it economically weak for high-frequency mean reversion \parencite{krauss2017statarb}.

\subsection{Cross-pair performance}\label{sec:result-baseline}

Figures~\ref{fig:audnzd-3m}--\ref{fig:noksek-3m} show the cumulative profit-and-loss (in basis points) of the four strategies, stacked across the three months, for each pair. The visual story is consistent across all nine pair-month panels: passive Buy \& Hold drifts mildly; the cointegration Baseline (rolling $z$-score with $z_q = 2.0$, $z_x = 0$) loses heavily; and the two HMM-aware strategies, AR and MS-AR, sit between the two, losing less than the baseline but never crossing into profit \parencite{gatev2006pairs,vidyamurthy2004pairs,krauss2017statarb}.

Three findings emerge clearly from the panels.

\textbf{(i) The cointegration baseline is unprofitable at realistic costs.} On every pair-month, the Baseline ends the period at a substantial negative cumulative bps. The mechanism is the same in all nine cells: the rolling $z$-score generates a steady stream of round-trips ($\sim 200$--$1{,}100$ per month) at a small per-trade alpha, and the half-spread plus commission per flip exceeds that alpha on average. This is the standard cost-of-mean-reversion-on-FX result of \textcite{krauss2017statarb}: the textbook signal carries information, but not enough to clear basic frictions at tick scale \parencite{lopezdeprado2018advances}. Sharpe ratios for the Baseline are large and negative across the board.

\textbf{(ii) The HMM gate is a defensive filter, not a return enhancer.} Across all three pairs and all three months, both regime-aware strategies sharply reduce market exposure (typically from $\sim 30\%$ for the Baseline to $5$--$15\%$ for AR and $6$--$17\%$ for MS-AR) and the round-trip count (typically by a factor of three to five). The result is that the cumulative-PnL curves of AR and MS-AR are pulled toward zero relative to the Baseline---in the language of \textcite{hamilton1989new} and \textcite{krolzig1997msvar}, the HMM correctly identifies and side-steps high-variance, low-mean-reversion episodes of the spread. Annual volatility and Ulcer index fall in tandem with exposure. What the gate does \emph{not} do is convert reduced exposure into a positive risk-adjusted return: Sharpe ratios for AR and MS-AR are still strongly negative, just less negative than for the Baseline \parencite{sharpe1994ratio,sortino1991downside}. The gate buys insurance, not alpha.

\textbf{(iii) MS-AR does not earn its complexity over AR.} The continuous, regime-weighted entry threshold of \eqref{eq:msarthreshold} and the binary danger gate of \eqref{eq:argate} produce nearly indistinguishable equity curves on every panel. Where they differ, MS-AR is mildly \emph{worse}: the softened threshold admits a few more entries than the binary gate, and at this cost level those marginal entries are net-negative. The extra free parameter $z_v$ therefore fails to justify itself at this resolution, consistent with the warning in \textcite{cappe2005} that adding smoothness to a regime decision rule is only beneficial when the marginal entries it admits are profitable in expectation.

\subsection{Passive drift as a reference}\label{sec:result-buyhold}

A Buy \& Hold position on the spread ($\pi_t^{\mathrm{BH}} = +1$, zero costs) is a useful passive reference \parencite{tsay2010}. In several months it is the only strategy with a positive cumulative PnL: notably on April 2025, where the spring 2025 tariff-driven episode produced strong directional moves on AUD/USD--NZD/USD and on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK, the passive drift earned what the active rules paid out in transaction costs. This is itself an instructive observation: at these frictions, taking many small mean-reverting positions on a noisy spread is dominated by simply doing nothing.

\subsection{Regime dynamics}\label{sec:result-regime}

Figure~\ref{fig:regime-dynamics} shows the per-day HMM fits for a representative month: regime-specific innovation variances $\sigma^{(k)}$ are clearly separated, persistence probabilities $p_{ii}$ are close to one for the mean-reverting state, and the smoothed posteriors $\gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}}$ track quiet versus turbulent periods within each session. These dynamics are qualitatively consistent with the textbook regime-switching picture of \textcite{rabiner1989tutorial,cappe2005}, and confirm that the model identifies what it is designed to identify; what it does \emph{not} do is turn that identification into additional return at this cost level.

\subsection{Synthesis}\label{sec:result-comparison}

Posed as in Section~\ref{sec:roleofhmm}---does the regime gate add value relative to the baseline, or mainly reduce exposure?---the empirical answer in this sample is unambiguous: the gate reduces exposure, but the underlying Baseline does not carry enough net alpha at this cost level for that reduction to translate into positive risk-adjusted returns. The HMM is therefore best read here as a \emph{costed risk-reduction device}: it cuts drawdowns and volatility, it makes the strategy easier to live with, and it provides interpretable, mathematically structured information about the local environment of the spread \parencite{cappe2005}. What it does not do, on its own, is manufacture profitability where the underlying micro\-structural signal is too weak to clear frictions.

This is a constructive negative result rather than a failure. It locates the boundary at which classical pairs-trading reasoning runs out of room on FX tick data and gives a clear direction for future work: combine the regime posteriors with an entry signal that is itself profitable after costs, rather than asking the HMM to rescue a baseline that is not \parencite{krauss2017statarb,avellaneda2010statarb}.
