\section{Empirical results and discussion}\label{sec:result}

This section reports the empirical results. Following the structure set up in Sections~\ref{sec:pairsbaseline}--\ref{sec:regimemodels}, we first describe the data and pair-level diagnostics; we then report the baseline back-test performance for the three currency-pair constructions; and we then ask whether the regime-aware extensions (AR-gated and MS-AR) improve on the baseline. All results are reported net of the transaction-cost assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:costs} and under the walk-forward protocol of Section~\ref{sec:walkforward}. Detailed per-pair tearsheets are collected in the appendix.

\subsection{Cointegration and walk-forward optimisation}\label{sec:result-coint}

Table~\ref{tab:cointegration} combines the main cointegration and walk-forward optimisation results. The full-sample cointegration tests are not sufficient on their own: AUD/USD--NZD/USD and GBP/USD--EUR/USD do not reject the no-cointegration null at conventional levels, but both show intermittent local mean reversion in rolling windows; EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK rejects the full-sample null, but its estimated half-life is extremely long, making the relationship economically weak for high-frequency trading.

The walk-forward results reinforce this distinction. AUD/USD--NZD/USD produces the strongest out-of-sample profile, with $17$ positive windows out of $21$ and an average out-of-sample (OOS) return of $2.65\%$ per window. GBP/USD--EUR/USD is profitable but weaker, with $11$ positive windows and a mean OOS return of $0.45\%$. EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK fails completely, producing zero positive OOS windows and an average OOS return of $-4.19\%$.

\subsection{Baseline back-test performance}\label{sec:result-baseline}

We now report the baseline pairs-trading rule of Section~\ref{sec:baseline} for each of the three pairs. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:roleofhmm}, this is the benchmark that any regime-aware extension must beat in order to justify the added complexity. The first three columns of Table~\ref{tab:perf_main} (labelled \textbf{Base.}) summarise the baseline performance; the equity curves and drawdown paths are in Figures~\ref{fig:bt_audnzd_main}--\ref{fig:bt_gbpeur_main} in the appendix.

The baseline already separates the three pairs cleanly.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AUD/USD--NZD/USD.} The baseline is strongly profitable: total return of $9286.14$~bps over the two-year sample with an annualised Sharpe ratio of $6.88$ and a Calmar ratio of $21.40$. Every calendar month is positive ($100\%$ positive months), maximum drawdown is only $263.67$~bps, and market exposure is moderate at $60.97\%$. The cointegration $z$-score signal is, on this pair, a strong source of mean-reversion alpha.
    \item \textbf{GBP/USD--EUR/USD.} The baseline is profitable but mild: total return of $2059.43$~bps, Sharpe ratio of $2.19$, Calmar of $3.36$. Wins are modest ($\mathrm{AW} = 2.20$~bps), and exposure is high at $62.59\%$. The pair is the most highly correlated of the three but the cointegration spread itself is more directional, leaving less mean-reversion opportunity per unit of exposure.
    \item \textbf{EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK.} The baseline loses money: total return of $-14035.70$~bps, Sharpe ratio of $-10.63$, no profitable months at all. Maximum drawdown reaches $-14096.99$~bps. The pair's combination of weak dependence, slow half-life ($71.8$~bars median), and wide spreads ($3.89$~bps median round-trip) destroys any apparent statistical edge.
\end{itemize}

A first observation already follows. The baseline back-test is itself diagnostic: it tells us which pairs are economically tradeable under realistic frictions before any regime-aware machinery is applied. AUD/USD--NZD/USD passes, GBP/USD--EUR/USD passes more weakly, and EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK fails. The regime-aware extensions cannot recover a non-cointegrated, high-cost pair; what they can attempt is to improve the risk profile of the two pairs that the baseline already trades profitably.

Three observations follow from Table~\ref{tab:perf_main}.

\textbf{(i) The HMM is primarily a defensive filter.} Across all three pairs and both extensions, the regime gate sharply reduces market exposure (from $60.97\%$ to $45.01\%$ on AUD/USD--NZD/USD; from $42.70\%$ to $25.11\%$ on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK; from $62.59\%$ to $39.17\%$ on GBP/USD--EUR/USD) and the number of trades. Annual volatility is reduced in every case. The HMM is doing what it is designed to do: it is identifying high-variance episodes of the spread and pulling the strategy out of those periods.

\textbf{(ii) Defensive behaviour does not translate into higher risk-adjusted return.} On AUD/USD--NZD/USD, the Sharpe ratio falls slightly from $6.88$ (Base) to $6.46$ (AR) and $4.93$ (MS-AR); on GBP/USD--EUR/USD, it falls from $2.19$ to $1.24$. The regime gate also \emph{enlarges} the maximum drawdown on GBP/USD--EUR/USD, from $-372.00$~bps to $-629.09$~bps, by removing some of the profitable exposure that would otherwise have offset the losing periods. In other words, the HMM correctly removes both upside and downside; on the two pairs where the baseline already works, removing exposure on average removes more profitable exposure than dangerous exposure.

\textbf{(iii) On the broken pair, the HMM only limits the damage.} On EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK the MS-AR strategy reduces the loss from $-14035.70$~bps to $-8796.04$~bps and the maximum drawdown from $-14097$ to $-8838$~bps. The HMM is therefore useful as a damage-control device on this pair, but it cannot convert the strategy into a profitable one: the underlying signal does not exist.

\subsection{Strategy comparison and synthesis}\label{sec:result-comparison}

Bringing Sections~\ref{sec:result-baseline} and~\ref{sec:result-regime} together yields a coherent picture of what the HMM contributes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The HMM identifies high-risk market states.} The smoothed regime posteriors, the regime-specific innovation variances, and the regime-conditional return distributions all behave qualitatively as one would expect: the MR regime is associated with low $\sigma^{(k)}$, fast mean reversion ($|\rho^{(k)}|$ small), and tight $z$-score ranges, while the DR regime is associated with high $\sigma^{(k)}$, $|\rho^{(k)}|$ close to unity, and wide $z$-score ranges. Figure~\ref{fig:regime-dynamics} in the appendix illustrates this on the three pairs.
    \item \textbf{The identified regimes are not perfectly aligned with profitable trading opportunities.} On the two profitable pairs, removing the high-variance regime removes profitable trades as well as losing ones. The risk-adjusted ratios are correspondingly degraded by the gate, not improved.
    \item \textbf{The MS-AR variant trades less than the AR-gated variant, but its threshold widening does not add value on top of simply removing exposure.} On AUD/USD--NZD/USD, MS-AR has the lowest Sharpe ($4.93$) of the three strategies; on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK, MS-AR has the smallest loss but also the smallest information ratio; on GBP/USD--EUR/USD, MS-AR's Sharpe falls below the AR-gated variant. The continuous probability-weighting of \eqref{eq:msarthreshold} therefore looks more like a smoothed version of the AR gate than a genuinely different signal.
    \item \textbf{The economic test of the HMM is therefore comparative.} Posed as in Section~\ref{sec:roleofhmm}, the question was: does the regime gate add value relative to the baseline, or does it mainly reduce exposure without improving risk-adjusted performance? The empirical answer is that, in this sample and under these costs, the gate primarily reduces exposure. It is a valuable diagnostic and a useful defensive tool, but not, on its own, a source of additional risk-adjusted return.
\end{itemize}

This is a constructive answer rather than a negative one. The HMM provides interpretable, mathematically structured information about the local environment of the spread; what it does \emph{not} do is automatically convert that information into superior trading performance. A natural direction for future work, discussed briefly in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}, is to combine the regime posteriors with a different entry signal (for example, a momentum signal active in the drifting regime) rather than only as a gate on a mean-reversion signal. That direction lies outside the scope of the present thesis, whose aim is to establish what the HMM contributes in a clean, single-signal pairs-trading setting.
