\section{Empirical results and discussion}\label{sec:result}

This section reports the empirical results. Following the structure of Sections~\ref{sec:pairsbaseline}--\ref{sec:regimemodels}, we first inspect the data and pair-level diagnostics, then back-test the baseline rule, then add the regime-aware extensions. All numbers are reported net of the transaction-cost assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:walkforward} \parencite{aldridge2013hft,lopezdeprado2018advances}.

\subsection{Data and pair diagnostics}\label{sec:result-pairdiag}

The three pairs differ substantially in liquidity and round-trip cost \parencite{roll1984spread,hasbrouck2007microstructure}: GBP/USD--EUR/USD is the cheapest, AUD/USD--NZD/USD is moderately liquid, and EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK is materially more expensive. All six leg-level return series are strongly non-Gaussian, with high kurtosis and significant Ljung--Box statistics on squared returns, consistent with conditional heteroskedasticity \parencite{engle1982arch,bollerslev1986garch,cont2001empirical}.

Rolling cointegration tests \parencite{engle1987cointegration,dickey1979distribution,said1984testing} reject the no-cointegration null only intermittently for AUD/USD--NZD/USD and GBP/USD--EUR/USD; EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK rejects globally but has a very long estimated half-life, which makes it economically weak for high-frequency mean reversion \parencite{krauss2017statarb}.

\subsection{Baseline back-test performance}\label{sec:result-baseline}

We back-test the baseline rule \eqref{eq:baselinerule} under the walk-forward protocol of Section~\ref{sec:walkforward}. This is the benchmark that any regime-aware extension must beat to justify the added complexity \parencite{gatev2006pairs,vidyamurthy2004pairs}.

The baseline separates the three pairs cleanly. AUD/USD--NZD/USD is strongly profitable on a risk-adjusted basis, with a high Sharpe ratio and a small maximum drawdown \parencite{sharpe1994ratio,magdonismail2004drawdown}. GBP/USD--EUR/USD is mildly profitable, with modest Sharpe and Calmar ratios. EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK loses money across the sample: weak rolling cointegration and wide spreads destroy any apparent statistical edge, exactly the scenario \textcite{krauss2017statarb} flag for high-cost statistical-arbitrage pairs.

This is itself a useful first result: the baseline back-test is a tradeability filter. Two of the three pairs pass; one fails, before any regime-aware machinery is applied.

\subsection{Regime-aware extensions}\label{sec:result-regime}

We add the AR-gated and MS-AR strategies of Section~\ref{sec:regimestrategies} on top of the baseline. Both reuse the baseline's directional rule and only change when it is allowed to trade, so any difference in performance is attributable to the regime gate alone.

Three findings emerge.

\textbf{(i) The HMM functions as a defensive filter.} Across all three pairs, the regime gate sharply reduces market exposure, the number of trades, and annual volatility. The HMM is doing what \textcite{hamilton1989new} and \textcite{krolzig1997msvar} would predict it should do: it identifies high-variance episodes of the spread, and the strategy spends less time in the market during those episodes.

\textbf{(ii) Defensive behaviour does not translate into higher risk-adjusted return.} On the two pairs where the baseline is already profitable, the Sharpe ratio falls when the gate is added: removing the high-variance regime removes profitable exposure as well as dangerous exposure. This is the empirical version of an old observation about regime models: the regime that is bad for variance is not always bad for expected return \parencite{cappe2005,rabiner1989tutorial}. On GBP/USD--EUR/USD the gate even \emph{increases} the maximum drawdown, by stripping out profitable exposure that would otherwise have offset losing periods.

\textbf{(iii) On the broken pair, the HMM only limits the damage.} On EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK the regime gate reduces the size of the loss and the maximum drawdown but cannot manufacture a profitable strategy where the underlying microstructural signal does not exist \parencite{krauss2017statarb}.

\subsection{Synthesis}\label{sec:result-comparison}

Bringing these observations together: the HMM identifies high-risk states correctly, the smoothed posteriors and regime-specific variances behave qualitatively as expected \parencite{rabiner1989tutorial,cappe2005}, but the identified regimes are not perfectly aligned with profitable opportunities. The MS-AR variant produces a smoothed version of the binary AR gate and does not add value on top.

Posed as in Section~\ref{sec:roleofhmm}---does the regime gate add value relative to the baseline, or mainly reduce exposure?---the empirical answer is the latter, in this sample and under these costs. The HMM is a valuable diagnostic and a useful defensive device. It is not, on its own, a source of additional risk-adjusted return.

This is a constructive answer rather than a negative one. The HMM provides interpretable, mathematically structured information about the local environment of the spread; what it does not do is automatically convert that information into superior trading performance. A natural direction for future work, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}, is to combine the regime posteriors with a different entry signal (for example, a momentum signal active in the drifting regime, as in \textcite{avellaneda2010statarb}) rather than only as a gate on a mean-reversion signal.
