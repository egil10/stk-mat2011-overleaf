Three patterns show up in Tables~\ref{tab:result_pnl}--\ref{tab:result_activity} and repeat in every diagnostic below. First, the Baseline loses money on every pair--month. It generates between $186$ and $1{,}116$ round-trips, and its Sharpe ratios range from $-17$ on the cheapest cell to $-126$ on the highest-spread one. The rule does earn a small per-trade alpha, but it pays the half-spread plus commission on every flip, and at this resolution the alpha is not enough to cover that cost \parencite{krauss2017statarb}. Second, the HMM gate cuts exposure by roughly four to six times and cumulative loss by about $75$--$85\%$, but it never turns the strategy profitable. The AR gate brings exposure down from $27$--$36\%$ to $5$--$15\%$, and the MS-AR variant is one to three percentage points more permissive than AR on every cell. Third, passive Buy~\&~Hold beats all three active rules on Sharpe in seven of the nine cells, and it is the only positive number in any column on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK in December 2024 and April 2025 and on GBP/USD--EUR/USD in all three months.

Why does Buy~\&~Hold dominate? The reason is mechanical, not statistical. Buy~\&~Hold flips its position at most twice in a month (entry and exit), so it essentially never pays the half-spread plus commission of \eqref{eq:midprice}. The Baseline pays that cost on hundreds of flips per month. We also picked the three months precisely because all three pairs were actively moving, so the spread itself drifts visibly inside those months and a passive long-spread leg captures that drift for free. Buy~\&~Hold therefore wins on Sharpe in cells where the active per-trade alpha is small enough to be eaten by the spread cost \emph{and} the spread has enough directional drift in that month for a passive position to ride it; both conditions hold simultaneously on most of the nine cells in our sample.

\subsection{Pair-level diagnostics and cointegration structure}
\label{sec:result-pairdiag}

The three pair constructions cover a wide range of liquidity \parencite{roll1984spread}. GBP/USD--EUR/USD is the cheapest pair to trade, AUD/USD--NZD/USD sits in the middle, and EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK is the most expensive by a large margin. All six leg-level return series are strongly non-Gaussian, with high kurtosis and significant Ljung--Box statistics on squared returns, consistent with the volatility clustering and conditional heteroskedasticity commonly observed in financial returns \parencite{cont2001empirical}. The per-bar period-return panels in Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-period-returns} show that these heavy tails remain visible at the trade-relevant horizon.

For a relative-value strategy, however, the central pre-trade question is not whether the individual legs are Gaussian. The relevant question is whether the constructed spread has enough statistical structure to justify mean-reversion trading. The cointegration screener therefore evaluates each pair-month along two dimensions. First, the full-sample test asks whether the entire monthly episode supports a stable long-run relation. Second, the rolling-window diagnostics ask whether this relation is persistent through time, or whether apparent cointegration is only a local and unstable feature of the sample.

The results in \cref{tab:coint-audnzd,tab:coint-noksek,tab:coint-gbpeur} show that cointegration is episodic rather than universal. AUD/USD--NZD/USD has the cleanest full-sample evidence in December 2024, with a p-value of $0.0445$ and a comparatively short full-sample half-life of $228.1$ bars. EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK has the strongest rolling evidence in April 2025, where $23.1\%$ of windows reject at the 5\% level and $41.3\%$ reject at the 10\% level. GBP/USD--EUR/USD is generally the weakest cointegration candidate: its full-sample p-values remain high across all three months, and its rolling rejection rates are mostly close to zero. This is important because the later strategy results should not be interpreted as trades on permanently cointegrated assets. They are trades on spreads whose mean-reverting structure appears intermittently and must therefore be filtered, timed, and penalised heavily for transaction costs \parencite{dickey1979distribution,neweywest1987,seabold2010statsmodels,krauss2017statarb}.

A representative screener panel for AUD/USD--NZD/USD in August 2024 is shown in \cref{fig:cointegration-audnzd-202408}. The complete August 2024 screener panels for all three pairs are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-cointegration-screener}.

\subsection{Baseline and HMM gate side by side}\label{sec:result-strategies}

Figure~\ref{fig:audnzd-3m} shows the cumulative profit-and-loss of the four strategies stacked across the three months for AUD/USD--NZD/USD. The corresponding panels for the other two pairs are collected on a single symlog grid in Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-strategy-panels}. Passive Buy~\&~Hold drifts mildly, the Baseline (rolling $z$-score with $z_q = 2.0$, $z_x = 0$) bleeds steadily, and the two HMM-aware strategies sit in between, losing less than the Baseline but never crossing into profit.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/AUDUSD_NZDUSD_three_month_strategy_panel_symlog.pdf}
    \caption{AUD/USD--NZD/USD, three-month strategy comparison on a symmetric-log $y$-axis. Each panel stacks the four cumulative-PnL curves for one month under the half-spread plus $0.05\,\text{bps}$ commission cost model. The Baseline (blue) leaks steadily; the AR gate (green) and MS-AR threshold (red) trace a much flatter, less negative trajectory; Buy~\&~Hold (grey) is positive in April 2025.}
    \label{fig:audnzd-3m}
\end{figure}

Three forces drive the Baseline result. The rolling $z$-score generates roughly $200$ to $1{,}100$ round-trips per month per pair, and each round-trip pays $\text{HS}^A_t + \hat\beta\,\text{HS}^B_t$ plus commission \parencite{aldridge2013hft}. The per-trade alpha is small relative to that cost: the average winning trade is $1$--$1.5\,\text{bps}$ on AUD/USD--NZD/USD and GBP/USD--EUR/USD and slightly larger on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK, with comparable average losses (Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-tearsheets}). The profit factor sits between $0.21$ and $0.76$ in every cell, the standard cost-of-mean-reversion-on-FX result of \textcite{krauss2017statarb} at tick resolution. And the loss distribution is heavily left-skewed and fat-tailed: Baseline skewness ranges from $-0.29$ to $-13.0$ and kurtosis from $12.5$ to $308.6$, so a handful of trade-level disasters dominates cumulative outcomes. April 2025 EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK is the most extreme case, with $1{,}116$ round-trips, $-19{,}906\,\text{bps}$ realised, and a profit factor of $0.21$.

The HMM gate inherits the Baseline's entry direction and changes only when the rule is allowed to trade. The per-day Baum--Welch fits \parencite{baum1970maximization} separate the two regimes cleanly by innovation variance on every pair--day (Figures~\ref{fig:apx-audnzd-202408-markov-dynamics}--\ref{fig:apx-gbpeur-202408-markov-dynamics} in Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-markov-dynamics}). The MR state has the smaller $\sigma^{(k)}$ and higher persistence $p_{ii}$; the DR state has the larger $\sigma^{(k)}$ and smaller $p_{ii}$; both means $\mu^{(k)}$ are close to zero, as the cointegration interpretation of $S_t$ requires. This clean separation translates into a uniform drop in trading activity. The AR gate fires $G_t = 0$ in the drifting regime and shuts the Baseline off, pulling exposure from $27$--$36\%$ down to $5$--$15\%$. The MS-AR threshold of \eqref{eq:msarthreshold} softens the binary switch into a continuous threshold interpolating between $z_q$ and $z_v$, which is slightly more permissive: it raises exposure by one to three percentage points and round-trip count by five to ten. The same ordering shows up in risk: annualised volatility, max drawdown, and the Ulcer index of \textcite{martin1989ulcer} all fall in the order $\text{BL} > \text{MS} > \text{AR}$ on every pair--month (Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-tearsheets}). What the gate does \emph{not} do is turn this lower exposure into a positive risk-adjusted return: the AR Sharpe is strictly less negative than the Baseline Sharpe everywhere, by a factor of two to five, but it never crosses zero. Once we condition on the rare drifting-regime entries the surviving sample is dominated by tail events, and the kurtosis of the gated strategies is actually larger than the Baseline's. On this data, the HMM buys insurance, not alpha.

The continuous-threshold MS-AR adds one free hyperparameter, $z_v$, on top of the binary AR gate of \eqref{eq:argate}, so that in-between values of $\gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}}$ are used instead of being thrown away. The extra flexibility does not pay for itself. On every pair--month in Table~\ref{tab:result_pnl}, MS-AR loses slightly more than AR (for example $-731.3$ vs $-652.2\,\text{bps}$ on AUD/USD--NZD/USD April 2025): at $z_v = 3$ the continuous threshold admits a handful of marginal entries that the binary gate would have suppressed, and at our cost level those entries are net-negative. On two of nine cells MS-AR beats AR on Sharpe by a small margin, consistent with the warning of \textcite{cappe2005} that smoothing a regime decision rule is only useful when the marginal entries it admits are profitable in expectation. A BIC-style penalty \parencite{schwarz1978bic} on $z_v$, applied through a hyperparameter search such as Optuna \parencite{akiba2019optuna}, would already select against MS-AR here.

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:result-discussion}

The four strategies tell a consistent story. Buy~\&~Hold is the only strategy with positive cumulative PnL in a majority of pair--months, and the only one not paying a tick-level cost. Its positive outcomes (the largest is $+527.7\,\text{bps}$ on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK April 2025, on the same data on which the Baseline loses $-19{,}906.4\,\text{bps}$) reflect persistent directional drift in the spread rather than any traded signal \parencite{gatev2006pairs}. The Baseline is the textbook cointegration rule and the worst-performing strategy on every pair--month: the per-trade alpha is in the right direction (win rate is close to $40\%$, and several daily ledgers in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix} contain individual gross-positive days) but it does not clear the round-trip half-spread, reproducing the headline of \textcite{krauss2017statarb} on tick-level FX. AR delivers the gate's promise (four-to-six-fold cut in exposure and round-trip count, $75$--$85\%$ reduction in cumulative loss, proportionally smaller drawdowns and CVaRs); whether that counts as an improvement depends on the loss function, since on Sharpe it is still unambiguously bad. MS-AR substitutes a continuous threshold for the binary switch and is mildly worse on PnL but marginally better on Sharpe in two of nine cells, so the extra parameter $z_v$ does not earn its keep at this cost level.

The qualitative pattern was partly expected: high-frequency FX is well known to be cost-dominated \parencite{krauss2017statarb}, and the conjecture from Section~\ref{sec:roleofhmm} that the HMM ``decides when not to trade, not what to trade'' lines up with the data. Less expected is the size of the Buy~\&~Hold advantage in directional regimes like April 2025, and how cleanly the pair-level cost ordering shows up across all nine cells: GBP/EUR (cheapest, smallest loss), AUD/NZD (intermediate), EUR/NOK--SEK (most expensive, largest loss). That ordering is monotone in every month, and is the cleanest causal statement we can make from this data: the Baseline's loss is, to leading order, the round-trip half-spread times the number of round-trips. The question posed in Section~\ref{sec:roleofhmm}, whether the regime gate adds value over the baseline or only reduces exposure, therefore has a clear answer. The gate reduces exposure, but the Baseline does not carry enough net alpha at this cost level for that reduction to translate into a positive risk-adjusted return. The HMM is best read as a costed risk-reduction device that locates the point at which classical pairs-trading reasoning runs out of room on FX tick data. The natural next step is to pair the regime posteriors with an entry signal that is itself profitable after costs (a momentum signal in the spirit of \textcite{jegadeeshtitman1993}, for example) rather than asking the HMM to rescue a baseline that is not.
