\section{Empirical results and discussion}\label{sec:result}

This section reports the empirical results. All numbers are net of the half-spread-plus-commission cost model of Section~\ref{sec:walkforward}, with the per-leg half-spread of \eqref{eq:midprice} paired with a fixed commission of $0.05\,\text{bps}$ per flip and a per-day fit. For each of the three pair constructions we report three monthly episodes side by side---\emph{August 2024}, \emph{December 2024}, and \emph{April 2025}---to keep cross-episode robustness visible at all times \parencite{lopezdeprado2018advances}. The full pair--month tearsheets, daily PnL ledgers, and per-pair figure-level diagnostics are in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}; the discussion below cites the appendix tables directly rather than reproducing them.

Three patterns are visible directly in Table~\ref{tab:result_headline} and recur in every diagnostic below. \textbf{(i)} The Baseline loses on every pair--month: between $186$ and $1{,}116$ round-trips, Sharpe ratios from $-17$ to $-126$. The rule earns a small per-trade alpha but pays the half-spread plus commission on every flip, and at this resolution the alpha does not clear the friction \parencite{krauss2017statarb,roll1984spread,lopezdeprado2018advances}. \textbf{(ii)} Regime gating cuts exposure $\sim 4$--$6\times$ and losses $\sim 5\times$ but never turns positive; AR pulls exposure from $27$--$36\%$ down to $5$--$15\%$ and MS-AR is $1$--$3$ percentage points more permissive. \textbf{(iii)} Buy~\&~Hold dominates the active rules on a Sharpe basis in seven of the nine cells; passive drift is the only positive number in any column on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK December 2024 and April 2025, and on GBP/USD--EUR/USD in all three months.

\subsection{Pair-level diagnostics and heavy tails}\label{sec:result-pairdiag}

The three pair constructions span a deliberate range of liquidity \parencite{roll1984spread,hasbrouck2007microstructure}: GBP/USD--EUR/USD is cheapest, AUD/USD--NZD/USD intermediate, EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK materially more expensive. All six leg-level return series are strongly non-Gaussian, with high kurtosis and significant Ljung--Box statistics on squared returns \parencite{engle1982arch,bollerslev1986garch,cont2001empirical}. Rolling Augmented Dickey--Fuller tests \parencite{engle1987cointegration,dickey1979distribution,said1984testing}, computed with \texttt{statsmodels} \parencite{seabold2010statsmodels}, reject the no-cointegration null intermittently for AUD/USD--NZD/USD and GBP/USD--EUR/USD; EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK rejects globally but with a long implied half-life, which makes it economically weak for high-frequency mean reversion \parencite{krauss2017statarb}.

The shape of the per-bar spread-return distribution makes the friction story concrete. Figure~\ref{fig:res-audnzd-rd-grid} reports, for AUD/USD--NZD/USD across the three episodes, the density on linear (top) and log (bottom) $y$-axes. The linear panels show a sharply peaked, near-zero-centred bulk; the log panels reveal Gaussian-incompatible tails at three or more standard deviations \parencite{cont2001empirical}. The corresponding grids for the other two pairs are in Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-return-distributions}; on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK the log-tail panels are visibly the widest, a precursor to the largest active-strategy losses below.

\subsection{Baseline under realistic frictions}\label{sec:result-baseline}

Figure~\ref{fig:audnzd-3m} shows the cumulative profit-and-loss (basis points) of the four strategies, stacked across the three months, for AUD/USD--NZD/USD; the corresponding panels for the two other pairs are collected on a single symlog grid in Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-strategy-panels}. Passive Buy~\&~Hold drifts mildly; the Baseline (rolling $z$-score with $z_q = 2.0$, $z_x = 0$) bleeds steadily; and the HMM-aware strategies, AR and MS-AR, sit between the two, losing less than the Baseline but never crossing into profit.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/AUDUSD_NZDUSD_three_month_strategy_panel_symlog.pdf}
    \caption{AUD/USD--NZD/USD, three-month strategy comparison on a symmetric-log $y$-axis. Each panel stacks the four cumulative-PnL curves for one month under the half-spread $+$ $0.05\,\text{bps}$ commission cost model. The Baseline (blue) leaks steadily; the AR gate (green) and MS-AR threshold (red) trace a much flatter, less negative trajectory; Buy~\&~Hold (grey) is positive in April 2025.}
    \label{fig:audnzd-3m}
\end{figure}

Three forces drive the Baseline result. First, the rolling $z$-score generates frequent round-trips---between $\sim 200$ and $\sim 1{,}100$ per month per pair, scaling with pair volatility and bar count---each of which pays a cost of order $\text{HS}^A_t + \hat\beta\,\text{HS}^B_t$ plus commission \parencite{roll1984spread,aldridge2013hft,lopezdeprado2018advances}. Second, the per-trade alpha is small relative to the friction: the Baseline's average winning trade is $1$--$1.5\,\text{bps}$ on AUD/USD--NZD/USD and GBP/USD--EUR/USD and slightly larger on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK; its average losing trade is comparable in magnitude (Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-tearsheets}), and the profit factor sits between $0.21$ and $0.76$ in every cell, well below the break-even level of one---the standard cost-of-mean-reversion-on-FX result of \textcite{krauss2017statarb} at tick resolution. Third, the loss is heavily left-skewed and fat-tailed: Baseline skewness ranges from $-0.29$ to $-13.0$ and kurtosis from $12.5$ to $308.6$, so a handful of trade-level disasters dominate cumulative outcomes. April 2025 EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK is the extreme: $1{,}116$ round-trips, $-19{,}906\,\text{bps}$ realised, and a profit factor of $0.21$.

\subsection{The HMM gate as a defensive filter}\label{sec:result-gate}

Both regime-aware strategies inherit the Baseline's entry direction; the only thing they change is \emph{when} the rule is allowed to trade. Figure~\ref{fig:regime-dynamics} confirms that, on a representative day, the EM/Baum--Welch fit \parencite{baum1970maximization,dempster1977,rabiner1989tutorial} separates the two regimes by innovation variance: the mean-reverting (MR) state has the smaller $\sigma^{(k)}$ and the higher persistence $p_{ii}$, the drifting (DR) state has the larger $\sigma^{(k)}$ and the smaller $p_{ii}$, and both means $\mu^{(k)}$ are close to zero, as required for the cointegration interpretation of $S_t$. Remaining pair--day fits are in Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-markov-dynamics}; their qualitative behaviour is consistent with the textbook regime-switching picture of \textcite{hamilton1989new,krolzig1997msvar,cappe2005}.

Regime-specific innovation variances $\sigma^{(k)}$, persistence probabilities $p_{ii}$, and regime means $\mu^{(k)}$ are well separated and stable across days. The MR regime has the smaller $\sigma^{(k)}$ and the higher $p_{ii}$; the DR regime has the larger $\sigma^{(k)}$ and the smaller $p_{ii}$.}
    \label{fig:regime-dynamics}

This clean separation translates into a uniform reduction in trading activity. The AR gate fires $G_t = 0$ during the drifting regime and shuts the Baseline off: exposure falls from $27$--$36\%$ for the Baseline to $5$--$15\%$ for AR. The MS-AR threshold of \eqref{eq:msarthreshold} softens the binary switch into a continuous threshold that interpolates between $z_q$ and $z_v$ with $\gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}}$, and as a consequence is slightly more permissive: it admits a few more entries than AR, raising exposure by $1$--$3$ percentage points and round-trip count by $5$--$10$. The same hierarchy is visible in volatility and drawdown: annualised volatility, max drawdown, and Ulcer index of \textcite{martin1989ulcer} all fall in the order $\text{BL} > \text{MS} > \text{AR}$ on every pair--month (Appendix~\ref{sec:apx-tearsheets}). What the gate does \emph{not} do is convert reduced exposure into positive risk-adjusted return: the AR Sharpe is strictly less negative than the Baseline Sharpe everywhere---by a factor of two to five---but it never crosses zero. Once we condition on the rare drifting-regime entries, the surviving sample is dominated by tail events and the kurtosis of the gated strategies is in fact \emph{larger} than the Baseline's. The HMM, on this data, buys insurance, not alpha \parencite{sharpe1994ratio,sortino1991downside}.

\subsection{AR versus MS-AR: complexity for free?}\label{sec:result-msar}

The continuous-threshold MS-AR strategy of \eqref{eq:msarthreshold} adds one free hyperparameter, $z_v$, on top of the binary AR gate of \eqref{eq:argate}. The motivation in Section~\ref{sec:regimestrategies} was that a soft threshold could exploit the in-between values of the regime posterior $\gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}}$ rather than throwing them away. The empirical answer is that the additional flexibility does not pay for itself in this sample. On every pair--month in Table~\ref{tab:result_headline}, MS-AR loses slightly more than AR (e.g.\ $-731\,\text{bps}$ vs $-652\,\text{bps}$ on AUD/USD--NZD/USD April 2025, $-2{,}491\,\text{bps}$ vs $-1{,}817\,\text{bps}$ on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK August 2024). The mechanism is identical across pairs: at $z_v = 3$, the continuous threshold admits a handful of marginal entries that the binary AR gate would have suppressed, and at this cost level those marginal entries are net-negative. Conversely, on two of the nine cells MS-AR beats AR on Sharpe by a small margin---suggesting that the extra parameter is a small win on volatility-adjusted return but a small loss on cumulative return. This is consistent with the warning of \textcite{cappe2005}: smoothing a regime decision rule is only beneficial when the marginal entries it admits are profitable in expectation. Here they are not, and a Bayesian-Information-Criterion-style penalty \parencite{schwarz1978bic} on the additional parameter $z_v$ would already select against MS-AR in this back-test. It is a genuine, costed negative result on the extra parameter.

\subsection{Discussion: comparing the four strategies}\label{sec:result-discussion}

Putting Buy~\&~Hold, Baseline, AR, and MS-AR alongside each other yields a coherent picture.

\textbf{Buy~\&~Hold} is the only strategy with a positive cumulative PnL in a majority of pair--months. It is also the only one not paying a tick-level cost. Its positive realisations on EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK December 2024 and April 2025 ($+528\,\text{bps}$ on the same data on which the Baseline loses $-19{,}906\,\text{bps}$), and on GBP/USD--EUR/USD in all three months, reflect persistent directional drift in the spread itself rather than any traded signal. Because the spread is just a linear combination of two log-prices, passive drift is a perfectly valid candidate trade---and at this resolution, a stronger one than the textbook rule that the spread theory predicts \parencite{tsay2010,gatev2006pairs}.

\textbf{The Baseline} is the textbook cointegration rule and, on this data, the worst-performing strategy on every pair--month. Its average per-trade alpha is in the right direction (the win/loss size ratio is below one but the win rate is close to $40\%$, and several daily ledgers in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix} contain individual days where the Baseline is gross-positive), but it does not clear the round-trip half-spread on average. This is exactly the headline of \textcite{krauss2017statarb} on tick-level FX, reproduced here under a per-day fit with explicit half-spread accounting.

\textbf{AR} delivers what the gate is asked to deliver: a uniform $\sim 4$--$6\times$ reduction in market exposure and round-trip count, a $\sim 75$--$85\%$ reduction in cumulative loss, and a roughly two- to five-fold improvement in Sharpe. Its drawdowns, Ulcer indices, and CVaRs are all proportionally smaller than the Baseline's. Whether one considers AR an ``improvement'' depends on the loss function: on a pure cumulative-loss metric it is unambiguously better, on Sharpe it is still unambiguously bad, and on a hypothetical investor's loss it is still bad in absolute terms but much less catastrophic. The HMM's contribution here is interpretable risk reduction, not alpha.

\textbf{MS-AR} replaces AR's binary on/off switch with a continuous interpolation between $z_q$ and $z_v$. In this back-test it is mildly worse than AR on cumulative PnL across the board, and marginally better on Sharpe in two cells out of nine. The extra free parameter $z_v$ does not earn its keep. This is not a contradiction with the regime-switching literature \parencite{hamilton1989new,krolzig1997msvar,cappe2005}; it is a statement about marginal entries at this specific cost level.

\textbf{Was this expected?} Partly. That high-frequency FX is cost-dominated is well known \parencite{aldridge2013hft,hautsch2012econometrics,krauss2017statarb}; that the regime gate could not rescue an unprofitable signal was a conjecture made explicit in Section~\ref{sec:roleofhmm} (``the HMM decides when not to trade, not what to trade''), and the empirical results confirm it cleanly. What was less expected is the size of the Buy~\&~Hold advantage in directional regimes such as April 2025---and the cleanness of the pair-level cost ordering across all nine cells. The cost-ordered ranking of cumulative damage on the Baseline---GBP/USD--EUR/USD (cheapest, smallest loss), AUD/USD--NZD/USD (intermediate), EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK (most expensive, largest loss)---is monotone in every month and is the cleanest causal statement we can make from this data: the Baseline's loss is, to leading order, the round-trip half-spread times the number of round-trips, and the regime gate's value is the reduction in the latter.

\subsection{Synthesis}\label{sec:result-synthesis}

Posed as in Section~\ref{sec:roleofhmm}---\emph{does the regime gate add value relative to the baseline, or mainly reduce exposure?}---the empirical answer is unambiguous: the gate reduces exposure, but the underlying Baseline does not carry enough net alpha at this cost level for that reduction to translate into positive risk-adjusted returns. The HMM is therefore best read here as a costed risk-reduction device: it cuts drawdowns and volatility, makes the strategy easier to live with, and provides mathematically structured, interpretable information about the local environment of the spread \parencite{cappe2005}. What it does not do, on its own, is manufacture profitability where the underlying microstructural signal is too weak to clear frictions.

This is a constructive negative result. It locates the boundary at which classical pairs-trading reasoning runs out of room on FX tick data and gives a clear direction for future work: combine the regime posteriors with an entry signal that is itself profitable after costs, rather than asking the HMM to rescue a baseline that is not \parencite{avellaneda2010statarb,krauss2017statarb}.
