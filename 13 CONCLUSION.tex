\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis has tested whether Hidden Markov Models add value to a classical pairs-trading rule on high-frequency FX tick data. The set-up is deliberately layered: the baseline is a textbook rolling $z$-score on a cointegration spread, and the HMM is used only as a regime gate that can switch the same baseline off. The three regime-aware strategies and the baseline are evaluated on the same nine pair--month cells, under a realistic half-spread plus commission cost model with per-day refits.

At a high level, the cointegration baseline is unprofitable on every pair--month at realistic frictions, the HMM gate cuts exposure and cumulative loss substantially but never restores profitability, the continuous MS-AR variant does not improve on the binary gate, and passive Buy~\&~Hold dominates the three active strategies in most cells. The Baum--Welch fits are interpretable on every pair--day, with well-separated innovation variances and persistent mean-reverting regimes, so the negative result on profitability is not driven by a misbehaving model but by the cost-to-alpha ratio of the underlying $z$-score rule.

Several caveats limit the scope of this conclusion. The exercise covers three pair constructions, three one-month episodes, $K = 2$ regimes, unit-size positions, and a cost model that omits latency and market impact; the three episodes were chosen for active price movement, which biases the comparison toward periods in which a passive long-spread position can drift directionally. The gating posterior is also smoothed in-sample within each day, so the gate sees slightly more information than a strict real-time filter would. The negative finding is therefore best read as a statement about the textbook $z$-score rule under realistic FX frictions, not about HMMs as a class.

Three extensions follow naturally. The most direct is to pair the regime posteriors with an entry signal that is itself profitable after costs (a momentum signal in the drifting regime is a natural candidate), rather than asking the HMM to rescue a mean-reversion signal that is already cost-dominated. A second is to replace the per-day smoothed posterior by a strict walk-forward filter using only past observations, which can only weaken the gate further and so tightens, rather than weakens, the negative conclusion. A third is an HMM--GARCH hybrid that lets the within-regime conditional variance evolve smoothly, a natural fit for the fat tails observed in the spread returns. The full implementation, daily ledgers, and per-pair tearsheets are released as the open-source repository \texttt{stk-mat2011} \parencite{furnes2026repo} as a starting point for follow-up work.
