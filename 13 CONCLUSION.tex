\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis has studied whether Hidden Markov Models add value to a classical pairs-trading rule on high-frequency currency tick data. The empirical exercise was deliberately layered: a single baseline signal (the rolling $z$-score of the cointegration spread) was first treated as a self-contained strategy and back-tested under realistic transaction costs and a walk-forward protocol; the HMM was then introduced on top as a regime gate, with two concrete instantiations (a binary AR gate and a probability-weighted MS-AR threshold) that leave the baseline's directional entry rule unchanged.

The main empirical findings can be summarised as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The baseline pairs-trading rule separates the three pairs cleanly.} AUD/USD--NZD/USD is strongly profitable (Sharpe $6.88$, Calmar $21.40$, no losing months); GBP/USD--EUR/USD is mildly profitable (Sharpe $2.19$, Calmar $3.36$); EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK is unprofitable in every back-tested window and exhibits weak rolling cointegration and high round-trip costs. This is a useful result on its own: the baseline back-test is itself a diagnostic for whether a pair is tradeable under high-frequency frictions.
    \item \textbf{The HMM is a defensive filter, not a return enhancer.} On all three pairs, the regime gate sharply reduces market exposure and the number of trades, and reduces annual volatility. On the two profitable pairs, however, it removes profitable exposure as well as dangerous exposure, and the Sharpe and Calmar ratios fall accordingly. On the unprofitable pair, the HMM only limits the damage; it cannot manufacture a signal that does not exist.
    \item \textbf{The MS-AR threshold smoothing does not add value over the binary AR gate.} The probability-weighted entry threshold of \eqref{eq:msarthreshold} produces a regularised version of the binary gate but does not change the qualitative picture and tends to underperform the binary AR gate on a risk-adjusted basis.
    \item \textbf{The model is interpretable.} The smoothed regime posteriors, regime-specific innovation variances, and regime-conditional return distributions are consistent with the financial intuition of a low-volatility mean-reverting regime and a high-volatility drifting regime. As a diagnostic for studying microstructural conditions through time, the HMM is informative even where it does not improve trading performance.
\end{enumerate}

The central contribution of the thesis is therefore not a claim that HMMs universally outperform simpler models, but a careful, costed, walk-forward test of \emph{when} regime awareness helps, \emph{when} it fails, and \emph{how} mathematical models behave when confronted with noisy, costly, non-stationary tick data. On the empirical evidence here, regime awareness primarily compresses the back-test along the exposure axis: less time in the market, less volatility, but also less return per unit of risk.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The exercise is restricted to three currency-pair constructions and a single two-year sample (2024--2025). The HMM is restricted to $K = 2$ regimes; higher-state models were not pursued because the information criteria did not consistently prefer them on this sample and because the marginal interpretability gain is small. The trading-rule space is restricted to long/flat/short positions of unit size; continuous probability-weighted position sizing was not investigated. Finally, the transaction-cost model is a half-spread plus a fixed commission, and does not include realistic latency, market impact, or partial fills.

\paragraph{Future directions.} The most natural extension is to combine the regime posteriors with a different entry signal in the drifting regime. The present construction always uses a mean-reversion signal; in the drifting regime, where mean reversion is by construction weak, a momentum or breakout signal would be a more natural complement to the baseline. A second direction is to pre-screen the candidate pair universe using the baseline back-test as a tradeability filter, applying the HMM only on the pairs that already pass; this avoids spending modelling effort on pairs like EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK that the underlying microstructure does not support. A third direction is to relax the strict half-spread cost model with explicit latency and partial-fill assumptions, to see whether the HMM's exposure reductions are more economically valuable in a model where transaction costs are themselves regime-dependent.
