\section{Methods}\label{sec:method}

In this section we develop the statistical and financial framework used to detect and trade short-term regimes in high-frequency currency-pair data. We begin with the data set and the pre-processing pipeline that turns raw bid--ask tick streams into synchronized observations. We then construct the cointegration spread that drives the trading signal. With the data in place, we introduce the autoregressive baseline, the general Hidden Markov Model, and the Markov-switching autoregressive specification that combines them. We discuss parameter estimation by the EM algorithm and how the recovered regimes are classified, before specifying the trading strategies, the walk-forward back-testing protocol, and the statistical and financial metrics used to evaluate performance.

\subsection{Data}\label{sec:data}

The empirical analysis is based on high-frequency foreign exchange data downloaded from Dukascopy. For each currency pair we collect separate bid and ask quote streams for both legs of the pair. The raw files are stored as monthly parquet files and loaded into Python through a reproducible data pipeline. The sample covers the period from January 2024 to December 2025, giving two full calendar years of tick-level FX observations.

The three currency-pair constructions used in the project are
\[
    \text{AUD/USD--NZD/USD},
    \qquad
    \text{EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK},
    \qquad
    \text{GBP/USD--EUR/USD}.
\]
These pairs were selected because they represent economically related exchange rates while differing substantially in liquidity, transaction costs, and empirical dependence. AUD/USD and NZD/USD are both Asia-Pacific currencies quoted against the US dollar. EUR/NOK and EUR/SEK connect two Nordic currencies against the euro but are less liquid and have materially wider spreads. GBP/USD and EUR/USD are highly liquid major pairs sharing the US dollar leg.

Using bid and ask data rather than only mid-prices is important in a high-frequency setting. At low frequencies, the spread may be treated as a small implementation detail, but at the tick level it is central to the economics of the strategy. A model may identify statistically significant mean reversion and still fail economically if the expected move is smaller than the cost of trading. For this reason the data set is constructed to preserve bid prices, ask prices, mid-prices, and spread-based transaction-cost estimates throughout the pipeline.

\subsection{Pre-processing}\label{sec:preproc}

The raw Dukascopy quote data are not used directly in the statistical models. Instead, they are transformed into synchronized pair-level observations through a custom Python pipeline. This step is important because high-frequency financial data are irregularly spaced, asynchronous, and strongly affected by bid--ask spreads and microstructure noise. The main pre-processing operations are described below.

\subsubsection{Bid--ask and mid prices}

For each currency leg, bid and ask quote streams are first sorted by timestamp. Since bid and ask updates do not necessarily arrive at exactly the same time, they are aligned using a backward as-of merge: at each timestamp, the most recent available quote is used, which avoids any use of future information.

For asset $x \in \{A, B\}$ we then synthesize a mid-price at every tick as
\begin{equation}
    M_t^{x} = \frac{B_t^{x} + A_t^{x}}{2},
\end{equation}
where $B_t^{x}$ and $A_t^{x}$ are the bid and ask quotes of asset $x$ at time $t$. The mid-price produces a single representative price series for each asset while still preserving the original bid and ask quotes for transaction-cost calculations.

\subsubsection{Bid--ask spread}

The bid--ask spread is central in HFT because expected profits are often small relative to trading costs. For each asset we measure the half-spread in basis points as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{HS}_t = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{A_t - B_t}{M_t}\times 10^{4}.
\end{equation}
The half-spread approximates the cost of crossing the market for one leg of the trade. Since pairs trading requires trading both legs, the combined spread cost is an essential component of the back-test in Section~\ref{sec:backtest}.

\subsection{Cointegration spread}\label{sec:spread}

Two non-stationary log-price series $\log A_t$ and $\log B_t$ are said to be cointegrated if there exists a hedge ratio $\beta$ such that the linear combination
\begin{equation}
    S_t = \log A_t - \beta \log B_t
\end{equation}
is stationary. The hedge ratio $\beta$ can be estimated globally by the Engle--Granger procedure, which combines an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression with an Augmented Dickey--Fuller (ADF) test on the residuals to verify stationarity. In a high-frequency context, however, $\beta$ is unlikely to remain constant over the full sample. We therefore model the cointegrating relationship as a slowly varying linear regression and estimate $\beta_t$ by rolling OLS with window length $W_\beta$:
\begin{equation}
    \beta_t = \frac{W_\beta \sum_{s} x_s y_s - \left(\sum_{s} x_s\right)\!\left(\sum_{s} y_s\right)}{W_\beta \sum_{s} x_s^{2} - \left(\sum_{s} x_s\right)^{2}},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_t = \frac{1}{W_\beta}\!\left(\sum_{s} y_s - \beta_t \sum_{s} x_s\right),
\end{equation}
where the sums run over $s \in \{t-W_\beta+1, \ldots, t\}$ and $x_s = \log B_s$, $y_s = \log A_s$.

The cointegration spread at bar $t$ is then
\begin{equation}
    S_t = \log A_t - \beta_t \log B_t - \alpha_t,
\end{equation}
and the spread return is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta S_t = r_t^{A} - \beta_{t-1}\, r_t^{B},
\end{equation}
where $r_t^{A} = \log A_t - \log A_{t-1}$ and similarly for $r_t^{B}$. The lag in $\beta_{t-1}$ ensures the hedge ratio is observable at the time the return is realized.

For the baseline strategy, which makes no use of regime information, the trading signal is the rolling $z$-score of the spread over a window of length $W_z$:
\begin{equation}
    Z_t = \frac{S_t - \mu_t}{\sigma_t},
    \qquad
    \mu_t = \frac{1}{W_z}\!\sum_{s=t-W_z+1}^{t}\! S_s,
    \qquad
    \sigma_t^{2} = \frac{1}{W_z}\!\sum_{s=t-W_z+1}^{t}\! \left(S_s - \mu_t\right)^{2}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Autoregressive baseline}\label{sec:ar}

When modelling a time series, a fundamental objective is to capture the linear dependence between current and past observations. The autoregressive model of order $p$, denoted $\mathrm{AR}(p)$, formalizes this intuition by projecting the current value of a variable $Y_t$ as a linear combination of its $p$ most recent past values:
\begin{equation}
    Y_t = c + \phi_{1} Y_{t-1} + \phi_{2} Y_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_{p} Y_{t-p} + \epsilon_t.
\end{equation}

In the context of financial returns, our benchmark is the simplest non-trivial special case: a single-regime $\mathrm{AR}(1)$ on the returns $r_t$,
\begin{equation}
    r_t = c + \phi\, r_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^{2}).
\end{equation}
Here $c$ is the constant intercept (from which the unconditional mean is $c/(1-\phi)$ for $|\phi|<1$), $\phi$ measures the strength and direction of the linear serial dependence, and $\epsilon_t$ is a Gaussian white-noise innovation. This model captures short-term serial dependence such as momentum or immediate mean reversion under a strict assumption of structural stability.

\subsection{Hidden Markov Models}\label{sec:hmm}

The main limitation of the single-regime $\mathrm{AR}(1)$ is precisely that assumption of structural stability: the parameters $c$, $\phi$, and $\sigma^{2}$ are taken to be constant. Real markets frequently undergo structural breaks and switch between distinct \emph{regimes}, such as transitioning from a low-volatility mean-reverting environment to a high-volatility directional one.

To capture this we introduce an unobserved discrete-time Markov chain $K_t \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$ representing the active regime at time $t$. The dynamics of $K_t$ are governed by a $K \times K$ transition matrix $\mathbf{P} = (p_{ij})$,
\begin{equation}
    p_{ij} = \mathbb{P}\!\left(K_t = j \mid K_{t-1} = i\right),
\end{equation}
with non-negative entries that satisfy the row-sum constraint
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j=1}^{K} p_{ij} = 1 \qquad \forall\, i.
\end{equation}

Conditioning the AR process on the hidden state $K_t = k$ allows the dynamics of the returns to adapt to the prevailing regime. The regime-switching $\mathrm{AR}(1)$ for returns is therefore
\begin{equation}
    r_t = \mu_k + \phi_k\, r_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_k^{2}).
\end{equation}
The parameter $\mu_k$ is a regime-specific drift, $\phi_k$ captures how short-term memory of the market changes with the environment, and $\sigma_k^{2}$ models regime-specific volatility, naturally accommodating the volatility clustering frequently observed in financial time series.

To fully specify the Hidden Markov Model we must estimate the parameter set $\theta$, which encapsulates both the state-transition dynamics and the regime-dependent autoregressive parameters:
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \left\{\, \pi_0,\; \mathbf{P},\; \mu_k,\; \phi_k,\; \sigma_k^{2} \;:\; k = 1, \ldots, K \,\right\},
\end{equation}
where $\pi_0 = (\pi_{0,1}, \ldots, \pi_{0,K})^{\top}$ is the initial state distribution with $\pi_{0,k} = \mathbb{P}(K_1 = k)$, $\mathbf{P}$ dictates regime persistence, and the remaining terms are the regime-localized AR parameters.

\subsection{Markov-switching AR on the spread}\label{sec:msar}

For pairs trading we apply the Markov-switching framework directly to the cointegration spread $S_t$ rather than to the returns of a single asset. Let $K_t \in \{1, 2\}$ be a latent regime indicator. We assume the joint generative model
\begin{equation}
    S_t \mid S_{t-1},\; K_t = k \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(c^{(k)} + \rho^{(k)} S_{t-1},\; \bigl(\sigma^{(k)}\bigr)^{2}\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}\!\left(K_t = j \mid K_{t-1} = i\right) = p_{ij}, \qquad p_{i1} + p_{i2} = 1, \qquad \mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix} p_{11} & p_{12} \\ p_{21} & p_{22} \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This is an $\mathrm{AR}(1)$ on the spread with all three parameters switching: the intercept $c^{(k)}$, the autoregressive coefficient $\rho^{(k)}$, and the innovation variance $\bigl(\sigma^{(k)}\bigr)^{2}$. When $|\rho^{(k)}| < 1$, the unconditional regime mean is
\begin{equation}
    \mu^{(k)} = \frac{c^{(k)}}{1 - \rho^{(k)}},
\end{equation}
which yields the equivalent mean-deviation parameterization
\begin{equation}
    S_t = \mu^{(k)} + \rho^{(k)}\!\left(S_{t-1} - \mu^{(k)}\right) + \varepsilon_t^{(k)}, \qquad \varepsilon_t^{(k)} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \bigl(\sigma^{(k)}\bigr)^{2}\right).
\end{equation}
Values of $\rho^{(k)}$ close to zero correspond to fast mean reversion within the regime, values close to one correspond to slow mean reversion, and values close to unity correspond to near-random-walk behaviour.

Before estimation we apply two pre-processing steps that improve numerical stability. We winsorize the spread at $\mathrm{median}(S) \pm 4\cdot\mathrm{std}(S)$, and we rescale it by a factor of $10^{4}$ so that the innovation variance is not numerically negligible.

\subsection{Parameter estimation}\label{sec:em}

The HMM parameters $\theta$ are estimated by maximum likelihood with the Expectation--Maximization (EM) algorithm \parencite{dempster1977}. The procedure alternates between:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{E-step.} Compute the smoothed regime posteriors
    \begin{equation}
        \gamma_t(k) = \mathbb{P}\!\left(K_t = k \mid S_1, \ldots, S_T;\; \theta\right)
    \end{equation}
    via the forward--backward algorithm.
    \item \textbf{M-step.} Update the regime-specific parameters $\bigl(c^{(k)}, \rho^{(k)}, \sigma^{(k)}\bigr)$ and the transition matrix $\mathbf{P}$ to maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood given the posteriors.
\end{itemize}
The two steps are iterated until the log-likelihood change between successive iterations falls below a small tolerance.

\subsection{Regime classification}\label{sec:regimes}

Once the parameters are estimated, the two regimes are labelled by their innovation variance. We define the mean-reverting (MR) regime as the one with lower volatility and the drifting (DR) regime as the one with higher volatility:
\begin{equation}
    k_{\mathrm{MR}} = \arg\min_{k}\, \sigma^{(k)},
    \qquad
    k_{\mathrm{DR}} = \arg\max_{k}\, \sigma^{(k)}.
\end{equation}
The in-sample regime probabilities used by the trading rules are the corresponding smoothed posteriors
\begin{equation}
    \gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}} = \mathbb{P}\!\left(K_t = k_{\mathrm{MR}} \,\big|\, S_1, \ldots, S_T\right),
    \qquad
    \gamma_t^{\mathrm{DR}} = 1 - \gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}}.
\end{equation}

A natural soft extension is to scale positions directly by the probability of being in the mean-reverting regime,
\begin{equation}
    w(K_t) = \mathbb{P}\!\left(K_t \in \mathrm{MR} \,\big|\, \mathcal{F}_t\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}_t$ denotes the information set available at time $t$. It is also natural to combine this with volatility scaling. In the strategies developed below, we use either a binary regime gate or a probability-weighted entry threshold rather than continuous position sizing.

\subsection{Model selection}\label{sec:modelselection}

After defining the structural family, the immediate empirical question is the choice of hyperparameters---most notably the number of hidden states $K$ and the autoregressive order $p$. Adding more states or lags will mechanically improve the in-sample likelihood but introduces the risk of overfitting to statistical noise rather than to genuine market structure. We resolve this trade-off by selecting models with information criteria that penalize complexity.

Let $m$ be the total number of estimated parameters, $T$ the number of observations, and $\hat{L}$ the maximized likelihood. The Akaike Information Criterion is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{AIC} = 2m - 2\ln \hat{L},
\end{equation}
and the Bayesian Information Criterion is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{BIC} = m \ln T - 2\ln \hat{L}.
\end{equation}
The AIC focuses on minimizing the information lost when the candidate model approximates the true data-generating process and is generally effective for optimizing predictive accuracy. The BIC scales its complexity penalty with $\ln T$, which is more aggressive in large samples and mathematically favours the most parsimonious model that adequately explains the data. During the hyperparameter sweep, models are estimated over a grid of $(K, p)$ values and the configuration that minimizes AIC and BIC is retained as the structural choice for the trading simulations.

\subsection{Trading strategies}\label{sec:strategies}

We compare four strategies that share the same cointegration spread $S_t$ and rolling $z$-score $Z_t$ as inputs but differ in whether and how they use the regime posteriors $\gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}}$ and $\gamma_t^{\mathrm{DR}}$.

\subsubsection{Buy \& Hold}

The do-nothing strategy is always long the spread:
\begin{equation}
    P_t^{\mathrm{BH}} = +1 \qquad \text{for every bar},
\end{equation}
earning $\Delta S_t$ at each step and paying \emph{no transaction costs}. Its role is diagnostic: if $\sum_t \Delta S_t > 0$ in a given month, the spread drifted in our favour and any active strategy must outperform that passive drift to add value; if $\sum_t \Delta S_t < 0$, the spread drifted against us.

\subsubsection{Baseline ($z$-score)}

The textbook pairs-trading rule uses two parameters: a quiet entry threshold $z_q$ and an exit threshold $z_x$. The position is updated bar by bar according to
\begin{equation}
    P_t =
    \begin{cases}
        +1       & \text{if } P_{t-1} = 0 \text{ and } Z_t < -z_q, \\
        -1       & \text{if } P_{t-1} = 0 \text{ and } Z_t > +z_q, \\
        \phantom{+}0 & \text{if } P_{t-1} \neq 0 \text{ and } |Z_t| \leq z_x, \\
        P_{t-1}  & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
A long position is opened when the spread is sufficiently below its rolling mean and closed when it returns toward zero; the short rule is symmetric.

In words, for a pair such as AUD/USD--NZD/USD the rule reads
\begin{equation}
    \left\{
    \begin{aligned}
        Z_t > +z_q       &\;\Rightarrow\; \text{short AUD/USD and long NZD/USD,} \\
        Z_t < -z_q       &\;\Rightarrow\; \text{long AUD/USD and short NZD/USD,} \\
        |Z_t| \leq z_x   &\;\Rightarrow\; \text{close the position.}
    \end{aligned}
    \right.
\end{equation}
For every unit of the first leg we trade $\hat{\beta}$ units of the second leg, so that the resulting portfolio is exposed to the relative movement between the two currencies rather than to the overall level of the FX market.

\subsubsection{Autoregressive (AR) with regime gate}

The AR strategy uses the same entry/exit \emph{directions} as the Baseline but adds a binary regime gate
\begin{equation}
    G_t = \mathbbm{1}\!\left\{\gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}} \geq 1 - \delta\right\},
\end{equation}
where $\delta \in (0,1)$ is a danger-threshold parameter. The modified position rule is
\begin{equation}
    P_t =
    \begin{cases}
        0                  & \text{if } G_t = 0 \quad (\text{panic liquidation, no new entries}), \\
        \text{baseline rule} & \text{if } G_t = 1.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The kill switch is binary: we either trade normally or sit in cash, with no middle ground. Moreover, the kill switch applies to existing positions, not only to new entries---if we are long the spread and the HMM suddenly flags danger, we close out and go flat at the next bar.

\subsubsection{Markov-Switching AR (MS-AR)}

The MS-AR strategy uses the same entry/exit directions as the Baseline but lets the entry threshold itself be dynamic and probability-weighted. With $z_v$ a wider ``volatile entry threshold'' that is active in the drifting regime, the effective entry threshold is
\begin{equation}
    z_q^{\mathrm{eff}}(t) = \gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}}\, z_q + \gamma_t^{\mathrm{DR}}\, z_v.
\end{equation}
The position rule is then
\begin{equation}
    P_t =
    \begin{cases}
        +1       & \text{if } P_{t-1} = 0 \text{ and } Z_t < -z_q^{\mathrm{eff}}(t), \\
        -1       & \text{if } P_{t-1} = 0 \text{ and } Z_t > +z_q^{\mathrm{eff}}(t), \\
        \phantom{+}0 & \text{if } P_{t-1} \neq 0 \text{ and } |Z_t| \leq z_x, \\
        P_{t-1}  & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
When the MR regime is highly probable, the threshold collapses to the tighter $z_q$ and the strategy trades like the Baseline; when the DR regime dominates, the threshold widens toward $z_v$, requiring a larger deviation before entering.

\subsection{Back-testing}\label{sec:backtest}

Back-testing is used to evaluate whether the model signals translate into economically meaningful trading performance. This step is important because a model can fit historical returns well without producing a tradable strategy. In high-frequency trading, expected profit per trade is often small, so even modest execution costs can eliminate an apparent statistical edge.

For this reason, the back-test includes realistic market frictions. The most important is the bid--ask spread itself: a trader generally buys at the ask and sells at the bid, rather than trading for free at the mid-price. Commissions and other execution costs are also included, since frequent position changes let small costs accumulate quickly. Financing and margin costs are relevant whenever the strategy holds short or leveraged positions.

The strategies are evaluated in a walk-forward structure, where parameters are estimated on past data and then applied to later out-of-sample periods. This protocol avoids look-ahead bias and provides a more realistic test of whether the Baseline, AR, and Markov-switching strategies would have worked under implementable trading conditions.

\subsection{Performance metrics}\label{sec:metrics}

To rigorously evaluate the trading strategies generated in the simulation we rely on a robust set of quantitative metrics. Simply observing the final account balance is insufficient; a proper evaluation requires us to understand the path taken to achieve those returns, the risks assumed along the way, and the underlying distributional behaviour of the system. These indicators allow us to objectively compare strategies, identifying their specific structural strengths, vulnerabilities, and overall viability in a live market context. Throughout this subsection we use the following notation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $R_t$: return of the asset or strategy at time $t$;
    \item $\mu = \mathbb{E}[R]$: mean return;
    \item $\sigma$: standard deviation of returns;
    \item $N$: total number of periods (or trades);
    \item $V_t$: portfolio value at time $t$, with running peak $P_t = \max_{s \leq t} V_s$;
    \item $R_f$: a benchmark ``risk-free'' rate.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Statistical metrics}

Statistical metrics form the foundational mathematical profile of a trading strategy. Rather than focusing directly on capital accumulation, they analyze the shape, spread, and probability distribution of the underlying return data. By examining how returns behave on a probabilistic level we can uncover hidden risks that standard equity curves might obscure---such as the likelihood of extreme market anomalies or structural asymmetries in the strategy's outcomes.

\textbf{Annual volatility} quantifies the expected yearly fluctuation in returns, providing a baseline measure of standard risk:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{ann}} = \sigma \sqrt{T}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Skewness} measures the asymmetry of the return distribution, helping to identify the likelihood of outsized positive or negative outliers:
\begin{equation}
    S = \frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(R - \mu)^{3}\right]}{\sigma^{3}}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Kurtosis} measures the ``fatness'' of the distribution tails, indicating the probability of rare, extreme market events:
\begin{equation}
    K = \frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(R - \mu)^{4}\right]}{\sigma^{4}}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Value at Risk} estimates the maximum expected loss over a specific time frame at a given confidence level $\alpha$, acting as a standard gauge of the worst-case scenario:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha} = -Q_{1-\alpha}(R).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Conditional Value at Risk} is the expected loss conditional on the VaR threshold being breached, providing a more comprehensive picture of extreme tail risk:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha} = -\mathbb{E}\!\left[R \,\big|\, R \leq -\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}\right].
\end{equation}

\textbf{Tail factor} quantifies the severity of tail risk by comparing the average extreme loss to the baseline extreme loss threshold:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{TF} = \frac{\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}}{\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Financial metrics}

While statistical metrics describe the mathematical probability of returns, financial metrics translate those probabilities into practical, capital-based outcomes. These indicators evaluate the actual equity curve, measuring how efficiently a strategy deploys capital, how it navigates periods of historical loss, and its absolute trading profitability. They contextualize raw returns against the specific drawdowns and volatility endured to achieve them, providing a holistic view of execution quality and the psychological ``holding stress'' the strategy would impose on an investor.

\textbf{Total return} is the overall cumulative growth or decline of the invested capital over the trading period:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{TR} = \prod_{t=1}^{N}\!\left(1 + R_t\right) - 1.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Annual return} standardizes the cumulative return into a yearly figure, allowing direct comparison across strategies and timeframes:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{AR} = \left(1 + \mathrm{TR}\right)^{1/Y} - 1.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Positive months} counts the number of profitable months, offering a straightforward measure of performance consistency over time:
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{pos}} = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \mathbbm{1}\!\left\{R_t > 0\right\}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Maximum drawdown} measures the largest peak-to-trough percentage drop in equity, revealing the worst historical losing streak:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MDD} = \max_{t}\!\left( \frac{P_t - V_t}{P_t} \right).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Maximum drawdown duration} is the longest time required to recover from a peak-to-trough loss, indicating capital recovery speed:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MDD}_{\mathrm{dur}} = \max\!\left(t_{\mathrm{recovery}} - t_{\mathrm{peak}}\right).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Ulcer index} combines the depth and duration of drawdowns into a single composite metric of holding stress, where $D_t = (P_t - V_t)/P_t$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{UI} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} D_t^{2}}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Sharpe ratio} measures excess return per unit of total volatility, serving as the industry standard for risk-adjusted performance:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SR} = \frac{\mu - R_f}{\sigma}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Sortino ratio} modifies the Sharpe ratio to penalize only downside volatility $\sigma_d$, making it suitable for investors who view only negative fluctuations as true risk:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Sortino} = \frac{\mu - R_f}{\sigma_d}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Calmar ratio} evaluates the annualized return relative to the maximum drawdown, highlighting the trade-off between reward and severe equity drops:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Calmar} = \frac{\mathrm{AR}}{\mathrm{MDD}}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Profit factor} is the ratio of gross profits to gross losses, a direct measure of absolute trading profitability:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PF} = \frac{\sum \text{Gross Profits}}{\sum |\text{Gross Losses}|}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Payoff factor} compares the average winning trade to the average losing trade, indicating risk-to-reward efficiency:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Payoff} = \frac{\text{Average Win}}{|\text{Average Loss}|}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Win rate} is the fraction of trades that result in a profit, reflecting the accuracy of the strategy's entry and exit signals:
\begin{equation}
    W = \frac{N_{\mathrm{wins}}}{N_{\mathrm{trades}}}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Market exposure} is the fraction of time capital is actively deployed in the market as opposed to sitting in cash:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Exposure} = \frac{T_{\mathrm{invested}}}{T_{\mathrm{total}}}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Average win} is the typical gain realized on a successful trade:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{AW} = \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{wins}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{wins}}} \text{Profit}_i.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Average loss} is the typical capital deficit incurred on an unsuccessful trade:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{AL} = \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{losses}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{losses}}} \text{Loss}_i.
\end{equation}
