\section{Methods}\label{sec:method}

In this section I introduce the methods. Namely, we start by introducing autoregressive models, before writing about Hidden Markov Models (HMM), introducing the data used in our simulations, while also presenting the statistical and financial metrics used to score the performance of our trading simulations. 

\subsection{Autoregressive models}

When modeling time series data, a fundamental objective is to capture the linear dependence between current and past observations. The general Autoregressive model of order $p$, denoted as $\text{AR}(p)$, formalizes this intuition by projecting the current value of a variable $Y_t$ as a linear combination of its $p$ most recent past values:

\begin{equation}
    Y_t = c + \phi_1Y_{t-1} + \phi_2Y_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_pY_{t-p} + \epsilon_t
\end{equation}

In the context of financial returns, we establish our benchmark using the simplest form of this process: a single-regime autoregressive model of order one, or $\text{AR}(1)$. This model serves as a robust baseline because it efficiently captures short-term serial dependence, such as market momentum or immediate mean-reversion. The $\text{AR}(1)$ process for returns $r_t$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    r_t = c + \phi r_{t-1} + \epsilon_t
\end{equation}

Here, $c$ is the constant intercept (from which the unconditional mean return can be derived as $\frac{c}{1-\phi}$), $\phi$ measures the strength and direction of the linear serial dependence, and $\epsilon_t$ represents the stochastic innovation term, strictly assumed to be independent and identically distributed as Gaussian white noise, where $\epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$.

\subsection{Hidden Markov Models}

While the standard $\text{AR}(1)$ model provides a solid baseline, its primary limitation is the assumption of structural stability; it assumes that the parameters ($c$, $\phi$, and $\sigma^2$) remain constant over time. Real-world financial markets, however, frequently undergo structural breaks and shift between distinct `regimes'—such as transitioning from a low-volatility bull market to a high-volatility bear market. 

To capture this dynamic behaviour, we extend our framework using a Hidden Markov Model (HMM). We introduce an unobserved, discrete-time Markov chain, denoted by $S_t \in \{1,\dots,K\}$, which represents the active market regime at time $t$. The probability of the market transitioning from one state to another is governed by a set of transition probabilities:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}(S_t=j \mid S_{t-1}=i) = p_{ij}
\end{equation}

where $p_{ij} \geq 0$ and the probabilities of transitioning from any state $i$ to all possible states $j$ sum to one. 
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j=1}^{K} p_{ij} = 1 \quad \forall i
\end{equation}

By conditioning our autoregressive process on this hidden state $S_t = k$, we allow the behavior of the returns to adapt to the current market environment. The regime-switching $\text{AR}(1)$ model is therefore defined as:

\begin{equation}
    r_t = \mu_k + \phi_k r_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \quad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_k^2)
\end{equation}

This formulation is significantly more flexible than the single-regime benchmark. The parameter $\mu_k$ acts as the regime-specific drift, $\phi_k$ captures how the short-term memory of the market changes depending on the environment, and $\sigma_k^2$ models regime-specific volatility, effectively accommodating the volatility clustering frequently observed in financial time series.

To fully specify this Hidden Markov Model, we must estimate the complete parameter set $\theta$, which encapsulates both the state-transition dynamics and the regime-dependent autoregressive parameters:

\begin{equation}
    \theta = \{ \pi, \mathbf{P}, \mu_k, \phi_k, \sigma_k^2 : k = 1,\dots,K \}
\end{equation}

where $\pi = (\pi_1,\dots,\pi_K)^\top$ is the initial state distribution representing the probability $\mathbb{P}(S_1=k)$ of starting in state $k$, $\mathbf{P} = (p_{ij})$ is the $K \times K$ transition matrix dictating the regime persistence, and the remaining terms represent the localized AR parameters for each respective state.

\subsection{Model selection}

After defining the structural framework of the Hidden Markov Model, the immediate empirical challenge is determining the optimal hyperparameters—most notably, the number of hidden states, $K$, and the autoregressive order, $p$. Adding more states or lags will invariably increase the model's fit to the training data, but doing so introduces the severe risk of overfitting, where the model captures statistical noise rather than genuine market structure. To resolve this tradeoff, we evaluate competing models using Information Criteria, specifically the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Both metrics penalize models for unnecessary complexity while rewarding goodness-of-fit.

The AIC focuses on minimizing the information lost when a given model is used to approximate the true data-generating process, making it highly effective for optimizing predictive accuracy. It evaluates models based on $m$, which represents the total number of estimated parameters in the model, and $\hat{L}$, which is the maximized value of the likelihood function for the given data. The AIC is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{AIC} = 2m - 2\ln(\hat{L})
\end{equation}

The BIC introduces a stricter penalty for model complexity by factoring in $T$, the total number of observations in the time series. Because the penalty term $\ln(T)$ scales with the sample size, the BIC aggressively penalizes overfitting in large financial datasets and mathematically favors the most parsimonious model that adequately explains the data. The BIC is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{BIC} = m\ln(T) - 2\ln(\hat{L})
\end{equation}

During our hyperparameter sweep, models are estimated across a grid of potential $K$ and $p$ values. The configuration that minimizes the AIC and BIC is selected as the optimal structural architecture for our subsequent trading simulations.

\subsection{Metrics}

To rigorously evaluate the performance of the trading strategies generated in this simulation, we must rely on a robust set of quantitative metrics. Simply observing the final account balance is insufficient; a proper evaluation requires us to understand the path taken to achieve those returns, the risks assumed along the way, and the underlying mathematical behavior of the system. In this section, I introduce the comprehensive suite of metrics used to score the models. These indicators allow us to objectively compare different strategies, identifying their specific structural strengths, potential vulnerabilities, and overall viability in a live market context. Before detailing the specific calculations, we define the following standard notations used throughout this section: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item $R_t$: Return of the asset/strategy at time $t$
    \item $\mu$: Mean return $\mathbb{E}(R)$
    \item $\sigma$ Standard deviation of returns
    \item $N$: Total number of periods or trades
    \item $V_t$: Portfolio value at time $t$, and $P_t=\max_{s \leq t}V_s$ is the peak value up to time $t$
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Statistical  metrics}

Statistical metrics form the foundational mathematical profile of our trading strategies. Rather than focusing directly on capital accumulation, these indicators analyze the shape, spread, and probability distribution of the underlying return data. By examining how returns behave on a probabilistic level, we can uncover hidden risks that standard equity curves might obscure—such as the likelihood of extreme market anomalies or structural asymmetries in the strategy's outcomes. The following metrics are utilized to quantify the fundamental risk and distributional nature of the strategy:

\textbf{Annual volatility} quantifies the expected yearly fluctuation in returns, providing a baseline mathematical measure of standard risk.
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{ann}} = \sigma\sqrt{T}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Skewness} measures the assymetry of the return distribution, helping to identify the likelihood of outsized positive or negative outliers.
\begin{equation}
    S=\frac{E[(R-\mu)^3]}{\sigma^3}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Kurtosis} measures the `fatness' of the distribution tails, indicating the probability of rare, extreme market events.
\begin{equation}
    K=\frac{E[(R-\mu)^4]}{\sigma^4}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Value at risk} estimates the maximum expected loss over a specific time frame at a given confidence level $\alpha$, acting as a standard gauge of the worst-case scenario.
\begin{equation}
    \text{VaR}_{\alpha} = -Q_{1-\alpha}(R)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Conditional value at risk} calculates the average expected loss assuming the VaR threshold is breached, providing a more comprehensive picture of extreme tail risk. 
\begin{equation}
    \text{CVaR}_{\alpha} = -E[R \mid R \leq -\text{VaR}_{\alpha}]
\end{equation}

\textbf{Tail factor} quantifies the severy of tail risk by comparing the average extreme loss to the baseline extreme loss threshold. 
\begin{equation}
    \text{TF} = \frac{\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}}{\text{VaR}_{\alpha}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Financial metrics}

While statistical metrics describe the mathematical probability of returns, financial metrics translate those probabilities into practical, capital-based outcomes. These indicators evaluate the actual equity curve, measuring how efficiently a strategy deploys capital, how it navigates periods of historical loss, and its absolute trading profitability. Crucially, these metrics contextualize raw returns against the specific drawdowns and volatility endured to achieve them, providing a holistic view of the strategy's execution quality and the psychological `holding stress' it would impose on an investor. The core financial scoring metrics used in our evaluation are defined as follows:

\textbf{Total return} represents the overall cumulative growth or decline of the invested capital over the entire trading period.
\begin{equation}
    \text{TR} = \prod_{t=1}^{N}\left( 1+ R_t \right) - 1
\end{equation}

\textbf{Annual return} standardises the cumulative return into a yearly figure, allowing for direct comparison across different strategies and timeframes.
\begin{equation}
    \text{AR} = \left( 1+\text{TR} \right)^{\frac{1}{Y}} - 1
\end{equation}

\textbf{Positive months} counts the total number of profitable months, offering a straightforward measure of performance consistency over time.
\begin{equation}
    \text{N}_\text{pos} = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}(R_t>0)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Max drawdown} measures the largest peak-to-trough percentage drop in equity, revealing the strategy's worst historical losing streak.
\begin{equation}
    \text{MDD} = \max_t\left( \frac{P_t-V_t}{P_t} \right)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Max drawdown duration} measures the longest time required to recover from a peak-to-trough loss, indicating capital recovery speed.
\begin{equation}
    \text{MDD}_{\text{dur}} = \max(t_\text{recovery} - t_\text{peak})
\end{equation}

\textbf{Ulcer index} combines the depth and duration of drawdowns into a single composite metric to evaluate the holding stress of the investment.
\begin{equation}
    \text{UI} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} D_t^2}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Sharpe ratio} measures the excess return generated per unit of total volatility, serving as the industry standard for risk-adjusted performance, where $R_f$ is a `risk-free' asset.
\begin{equation}
    \text{SR} = \frac{\mu-R_f}{\sigma}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Sortino ratio} modifies the Sharpe ratio to penalise only downside volatility, making it ideal for investors who view only negative fluctuations as true risk.
\begin{equation}
    \text{Sortino} = \frac{\mu-R_f}{\sigma_d}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Calmar ratio} evaluates the annualised return relative to the meaxmium drawdown, highlighting the specific tradeoff between reward and severe equity drops.
\begin{equation}
    \text{Calmar} = \frac{\text{AR}}{\text{MDD}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Profit factor} shows the total money made for every dollar lost, acting as a pure, direct measure of absolute trading profitability.
\begin{equation}
    \text{PF} = \frac{\sum\text{Gross Profits}}{\sum|\text{Gross Losses}|}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Payoff factor} compares the absolute size of the average winning trade to the average losing trade, indicating the system's risk-to-reward efficiency. 
\begin{equation}
    \text{Payoff} = \frac{\text{Average Win}}{|\text{Average Loss}|}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Win rate} calculates the percentage of trades that result in a profit, reflecting the core accuracy of the strategy's entry and exit signals. 
\begin{equation}
    W=\frac{N_\text{wins}}{N_\text{trades}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Market exposure} measures the fraction of time capital is actively deployed in the market as opposed to sitting safely in cash.
\begin{equation}
    \text{Exposure} = \frac{T_{\text{invested}}}{T_{\text{total}}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Average win} calculates the typical gain realised on a successful transaction.
\begin{equation}
    \text{AW} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{wins}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_\text{wins}} \text{Profit}_i
\end{equation}

\textbf{Average loss} calculates the typical capital deficit incurred on an unsuccessful transaction. 
\begin{equation}
    \text{AL} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{losses}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_\text{losses}} \text{Loss}_i
\end{equation}

\subsection{Data}

The empirical analysis is based on high-frequency foreign exchange data downloaded from Dukascopy. For each currency pair, I collect separate bid and ask quote data for both legs of the pair. The raw files are stored as monthly parquet files and loaded into Python through a reproducible data pipeline. The sample covers the period from January 2024 to December 2025, giving two full calendar years of tick-level FX observations.

The three currency-pair constructions used in the project are
\[
    \text{AUD/USD--NZD/USD}, 
    \qquad
    \text{EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK},
    \qquad
    \text{GBP/USD--EUR/USD}.
\]
These pairs were selected because they represent economically related exchange rates while differing substantially in liquidity, transaction costs, and empirical dependence. AUD/USD and NZD/USD are both Asia-Pacific currencies quoted against the US dollar. EUR/NOK and EUR/SEK connect two Nordic currencies against the euro, but are less liquid and have materially wider spreads. GBP/USD and EUR/USD are highly liquid major currency pairs sharing the US dollar leg.

Using bid and ask data rather than only mid-prices is important in a high-frequency setting. At low frequencies, the spread may be treated as a small implementation detail, but at the tick level it is central to the economics of the strategy. A model may identify statistically significant mean reversion and still fail economically if the expected move is smaller than the cost of trading. For this reason, the data set is constructed to preserve bid prices, ask prices, mid-prices, and spread-based transaction-cost estimates throughout the pipeline.

\subsection{Pre-processing}

The raw Dukascopy quote data are not used directly in the statistical models. Instead, the data are transformed into synchronized pair-level observations through a custom Python pipeline. This preprocessing is important because high-frequency financial data are irregularly spaced, asynchronous, and strongly affected by bid--ask spreads and microstructure noise. The main preprocessing steps are described below.

\subsubsection{Bid--Ask and Mid prices}

For each currency leg, bid and ask quote streams are first sorted by timestamp. Since bid and ask updates do not necessarily arrive at exactly the same time, they are aligned using a backward as-of merge. This means that, at each timestamp, the most recent available quote is used, avoiding any use of future information.

The mid-price is then computed as
\begin{equation}
    P_t=\frac{P_t^{\text{ask}}}{P_t^{\text{bid}}}
\end{equation}
which produces a single representative price series for each asset while still preserving the original bid and ask quotes for transaction-cost calculations.

For asset $X\in\{A,B\}$ we synthesis a mid price at every tick
\begin{equation}
    M_t^x = \frac{B_t^x+A_t^x}{2}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Spread}

The bid--ask spread is central in HFT because expected profits are often small relative to trading costs. For each asset, the half-spread is measured in basis points as
\begin{equation}
    \text{HS}_t = \frac{1}{2}\frac{P_t^{\text{ask}}}{P_t^{\text{bid}}}\times10\space000
\end{equation}

The half-spread gives an estimate of the cost of crossing the market for one leg of the trade. Since pairs trading requires trading both legs, the combined spread cost is an important part of the later backtest.

Two non-stationary log-price series $\log A_t$ and $\log B_t$ are cointegrated if there exists a hedge ratio $\beta$ such that the spread
\begin{equation}
    S_t = \log A_t - \beta \log B_t
\end{equation}
is stationary.

We model the cointegrating relationship as a slowly-varying linear regression of $\log A$ and $\log B$. We do not assume $\beta$ is constant, we rather estimate $\beta_t$ via rolling OLS with window size $W_b$.
\begin{equation}
    \beta_t = \frac{W_\beta \sum_{s=t-W_\beta+1}^{t}x_sy_s-(\sum_sx_s)(\sum_sy_s)}{W_\beta\sum_sx_s^2-(\sum_sx_s)^2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_t = \frac{1}{W_\beta}\left(\sum_sy_s-\beta_t\sum_sx_s\right)
\end{equation}
with $x_s=\log B_s$ and $y_s=\log A_s$

The cointegration spread at bar $t$ is then
\begin{equation}
    S_t = \log A_t - \beta_t \log B_t - \alpha_t
\end{equation}
and the spread return is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta S_t = r_t^A - \beta_{t-1}r_t^B
\end{equation}
where $r_t^A=\log A_t - \log A_{t-1}$ etc

For the baseline strategy which knows nothing about regimes the trading signal is the rolling z-score of the spread
\begin{equation}
    Z_t = \frac{S_t - \mu_t}{\sigma_t}
    \quad 
    \mu_t = \frac{1}{W_z}\sum_{s=t-W_z+1}^{t} S_s
    \quad 
    \sigma_t^2 = \frac{1}{W_z}\sum_{s=t-W_z+1}^{t}(S_s-\mu_t)^2
\end{equation}
with $W_z$ bars.

\subsection{Pairs trading}

We choose to financial instruments, as AUDUSD and NZDUSD in this paper. Let us define the spread $z_t$ as 
\begin{equation}
    z_t = p_t^{(1)} - \beta p_t^{(2)}
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ can be estimated by the Engle-Granger method, which means using an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression followed by an Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test on the residuals to verify that $z_t$ is stationary. It is also possible to esimate this directly in the HMM model, but easier to do so globally at first. 

Thereafter we can fit an AR- or ARCH-HMM on the spread-process
\begin{equation}
    z_t = \mu^k + \rho^k(z_{t-1}-\mu^k) + \varepsilon_t^k 
\end{equation}
where $\rho^k$ measures how fast we expect the mean reversion to be in regime $k$. Values of $\rho^k$ close to $0$ indicate a faster mean reversion, while values closer to $1$ indicate a slower mean reversion. Negative values of $\rho^k$ would indicate even stronger mean reversion but possibly less realistic empirically. 

The trading rule in pairs trading can be formulated as follows. In a drifting regime we do nothing. In a mean-reverting regime, say for regime $k=2$ we trade by.

\begin{equation}
    \left\{
    \begin{aligned}
        z_t > \mu^2 + c\sigma^2 
        &\Rightarrow \text{short AUDUSD and long NZDUSD}, \\
        z_t < \mu^2 - c\sigma^2 
        &\Rightarrow \text{long AUDUSD and short NZDUSD}, \\
        z_t \approx \mu^2 
        &\Rightarrow \text{close the position}.
    \end{aligned}
    \right.
\end{equation}

While for one unit of AUDUSD we trade $\hat{\beta}$ units of NZDUSD, such that the position in main is exposed to the relative movement between AUDUSD and NZDUSD, and not toward the general level in the currency market. 

For a soft HMM version the positions can be scaled with the probabilites of the model being in a certain mean-reverting regime
\begin{equation}
    w(s_t) = \mathbb{P}(s_t \space \text{is in a mean-reverting regime} \mid \mathcal{F_t})
\end{equation}
It may also be natural to scale the positions with estimated volatility.

\subsection{Back-testing}

Back-testing is used to evaluate whether the model signals translate into economically meaningful trading performance. This is important because a model can fit historical returns well without producing a tradable strategy. In high-frequency trading, the expected profit per trade is often small, so even modest execution costs can eliminate an apparent statistical edge.

For this reason, the backtest includes realistic market frictions. The most important is the bid--ask spread: a trader generally buys at the ask and sells at the bid, rather than trading for free at the mid-price. Commissions and other execution costs are also included, since frequent position changes can make small costs accumulate quickly. Financing and margin costs are relevant when the strategy holds short or leveraged positions.

The strategies are evaluated in a walk-forward structure, where parameters are estimated on past data and then applied to later out-of-sample periods. This avoids look-ahead bias and gives a more realistic test of whether the baseline, AR, and Markov-switching strategies would have worked under implementable trading conditions.

\subsection{Models}

\subsubsection{The Buy \& Hold model}

The do-nothing strategy, the position is always long the spread. 
\begin{equation}
    P_t^{\text{BH}} = +\space 1 \quad \text{for every bar} 
\end{equation}
and it earns $\Delta S_t$ every bar and pays \textit{no transaction costs.}

The purpose of the Buy \& Hold is diagnostic, if $\sum_t \Delta S_t > 0$ for a given month, the spread drifter in our favour and a trading strategy needs to do better than that passive drift to add value, and if $\sum_t \Delta S_t < 0$ the spread drifted against us. 

\subsubsection{The Baseline model}

The textbook $z-\text{score}$ strategy, with two parameters, $z_q = \text{``quiet entry threshold''}$ and $z_x = \text{``exit threshold''}$. 

Position rules, applied bar-by-bar:
\begin{equation}
    P_t = 
    \begin{cases}
        +1 & \quad\text{if}\quad P_{t-1} = 0 \quad&\text{and}\quad Z_t < -z_q \\
        -1 & \quad\text{if}\quad P_{t-1} = 0 \quad&\text{and}\quad Z_t > +z_q \\
        0 & \quad\text{if}\quad P_{t-1} = +1 \quad&\text{and}\quad Z_t \geq -z_q \\
        0 & \quad\text{if}\quad P_{t-1} = -1 \quad&\text{and}\quad Z_t \leq -z_q \\
        P_{t-1} & \quad\text{otherwise}\quad &
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{The Autoregressive (AR) model}

Here we use the same entry/exit \textit{direction} rules as the Baseline model with one extra binary condition, the gate
\begin{equation}
    G_t = 1 \{ \pi_t^{\text{MR}} \geq 1-\delta \}
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is the danger threshold parameter. The modified ruled is therefore 
\begin{equation}
    P_t =
    \begin{cases}
        0 & \quad&\text{if}\quad G_t=0 \text{(panic liquidation, no new entires)} \\
        \text{(same as baseline)} &\quad&\text{if}\quad G_t = 1
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The kill switch is binary, we either trade normally or sit in cash, with no middle ground. Moreso the kill applies to existing positions too, not just to new entreis, if we aer long the spread and the HMM suddenly flags danger, we close out and go flag at the next bar.



\subsubsection{The Markov-Switching Autoregressive (MS-AR) model}

Let us denote $S_t$ as the spread and $K_t\in\{1,2\}$ a latent discrete regime indicator. The joint generative model is
\begin{equation}
    S_t \mid S_{t-1}, K_t = k \sim N\left( c^{(k)} + \rho^{(k)}S_{t-1}, \left( \sigma^{(k)} \right)^2 \right)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    P(K_t=j \mid K_{t-1} = i) = p_{ij} 
    \quad
    P = \left( p_{11} \quad p_{12} \quad p_{21} \quad p_{22} \right)
    \quad
    p_{i1} + p_{i2} = 1
\end{equation}

This is an $\text{AR}(1)$ on the spread with all three parameters switching, the constant $c^{(k)}$, the AR coefficient $\rho^{(k)}$ and the innovation variance $\left(\sigma^{(k)}\right)^2$. 

The unconditional mean of regime $k$ when $|\rho^{(k)}|<1$ is 
\begin{equation}
    \mu^{(k)} = \frac{c^{(k)}}{1-\rho^{(k)}}
\end{equation}

We fit the maxmium likelihood with the $\text{EM}$ algorithm, which alternate between the E-step, where we compute regime posteriors $\gamma_t^{(k)}=\mathbb{P}(K_t=k|S_1,\dots,S_t)$ via the forward-backward algorithm. And the M-step, where we update the regime-specific parameters $\left( c^{(k)}, \rho^{(k)}, \sigma^{(k)} \right)$ and the transition matrix $P$ to maximise the expected complete-data log-likelihood given the posteriors. 

Winsorisation, we clip $S_t$ at $\text{median}(S)\pm4 \times \text{std}(S)$ before fitting. Also we scale by multiplying $S_t$ by $10^4$ before fitting. 

The regime classification labelling is done as either 
\begin{equation}
    \text{MR index} = \arg\max_k\sigma^{(k)}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{DR index} = \arg\max_k\sigma^{(k)}
\end{equation}

The in-sample regime probabilities are then found by
\begin{equation}
    \pi_t^{\text{MR}} 
    = 
    \gamma_t^{\text{(MR)}}
    =
    \mathbb{P}(K_t=\text{MR}|S_1,\dots,S_T)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \pi_t^{\text{DR}} = 1-\pi_t^{\text{MR}}
\end{equation}


Same entry/exit direction rules as Baseline, but the entry threshold itself is dynamic and probability-weighted
\begin{equation}
    z_q^{\text{eff}}(t) = \pi_t^{\text{MR}} \cdot z_q + \pi_t^{\text{DR}}\cdot z_v
\end{equation}
where $z_v$ is the ``volatile entry threshold''. The position rule is as follows
\begin{equation}
    P_t = 
    \begin{cases}
        +1 \quad&\text{if}\ P_{t-1}=0 &\text{and}\quad Z_t < -z_q^{\text{eff}}(t) \\
        -1 \quad&\text{if}\quad P_{t-1}=0 &\text{and}\quad Z_t > +z_q^{\text{eff}}(t) \\
        0 \quad&\text{if}\quad P_{t-1}=\neq0 &\text{and}\quad     |Z_t| \leq z_x \\
        +1 \quad&\text{otherwise}\quad &\text{}\quad         
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
