\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{The quantitative revolution}

Modern financial markets are mathematical objects. The trading pits, voice execution, and discretionary judgement of the past have been replaced by electronic limit-order books, automated market makers, statistical arbitrage engines, and latency-sensitive execution algorithms \parencite{lewis2014flashboys,aldridge2013hft,biais2015equilibrium}. Prices are no longer just economic summaries of fundamentals; they are also high-dimensional data streams generated by the interaction of agents, algorithms, inventories, order flows, and information shocks \parencite{ohara1995market,hasbrouck2007microstructure,hautsch2012econometrics}.

From a mathematical perspective, the deeper issue is not speed but non-stationarity: financial time series are noisy, adaptive, and adversarial, so any exploitable structure is weak, transient, and rapidly arbitraged away \parencite{cont2001empirical}. The trader is no longer simply asking whether an asset is ``cheap'' or ``expensive'', but whether a small statistical regularity can be detected, validated, traded, and risk-managed after costs \parencite{lopezdeprado2018advances,aitsahalia2014}. Jim Simons is a useful symbol of this view: a mathematician who treated finance as a signal-processing problem rather than as economic forecasting \parencite{zuckerman2019man}.

\subsection{FX microstructure and pairs trading}

The foreign exchange (FX) market is one of the most natural settings in which to study this problem: it is globally traded, electronically intermediated, and active nearly twenty-four hours a day. At low frequencies, exchange rates are well approximated by continuous diffusions \parencite{bachelier1900theorie,blackscholes1973pricing,merton1973theory}. At the tick level, however, prices move in small discrete increments, quotes update asynchronously, and the price path becomes a sequence of small jumps and microstructural frictions \parencite{roll1984spread,jacod2017}. Figure~\ref{fig:high-frequency-microstructure} illustrates this scale dependence on a single FX series: the daily view is approximately diffusive, the hourly view shows localised mean reversion, and the tick view is dominated by discrete bid--ask bounce. A single stationary Gaussian model does not capture this picture.

Currency pairs with shared economic or geographic exposure are natural candidates for pairs trading: rather than betting on the level of one exchange rate, one bets on temporary deviations between two related ones reverting to a common stochastic trend \parencite{gatev2006pairs,vidyamurthy2004pairs,avellaneda2010statarb,krauss2017statarb}. The three constructions used here (AUD/USD vs NZD/USD, EUR/NOK vs EUR/SEK, and GBP/USD vs EUR/USD) span a range of liquidity and dependence regimes.

\subsection{Non-stationarity and regimes}

The main statistical difficulty is non-stationarity: volatility clusters, correlations rise and fall, and liquidity varies intraday \parencite{engle1982arch,bollerslev1986garch,cont2001empirical}. A trading rule that works in one regime may fail in another. This motivates the language of market regimes: persistent latent states, each with its own statistical properties, switching according to some stochastic mechanism \parencite{hamilton1989new}.

I observe only the emitted data: returns, spreads, and trading outcomes. The natural modelling tool is therefore a Hidden Markov Model (HMM), which assumes that an observable process is driven by an unobserved Markov chain whose state determines the distribution of the observation \parencite{rabiner1989tutorial,cappe2005,krolzig1997msvar}.

\subsection{The role of the HMM in this thesis}\label{sec:roleofhmm}

The exercise is deliberately layered. I first build a single tradable signal, the rolling cointegration spread $S_t$ \parencite{engle1987cointegration,dickey1979distribution} standardised into a $z$-score $Z_t$, and use it inside a textbook pairs-trading rule that enters and exits positions on $Z_t$ alone \parencite{gatev2006pairs,vidyamurthy2004pairs}. This is the baseline, and it is back-tested first under realistic transaction costs so that it serves as the benchmark against which any added complexity must justify itself. I then introduce a Markov-switching AR(1) on the same $S_t$ \parencite{hamilton1989new,krolzig1997msvar} to recover a hidden chain $K_t \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$ representing the active regime, and use the HMM as a regime gate on top of the baseline signal rather than as an independent forecaster: the trading direction stays dictated by $Z_t$, and the HMM's role is to suppress or down-weight trading during high-volatility, weak-mean-reversion regimes. In short, the baseline gives the signal and the HMM decides when not to trade. Figure~\ref{fig:true-and-hidden-states} previews the regime-recovery step on a simulated two-regime AR(1): the hidden state is recovered cleanly from the observable series alone, which is the property that the gate exploits on the real spread $S_t$ later in the thesis.

Because the regime-aware strategies inherit the baseline's direction, any difference between them and the baseline is attributable to the gate alone, which is why baseline results are reported before regime-model results.

\subsection{Notation and abbreviations}\label{sec:abbrev}

A short reference list used throughout the thesis. Currency-pair constructions are abbreviated as AUD/NZD for AUD/USD--NZD/USD, EUR/NOK--SEK for EUR/NOK--EUR/SEK, and GBP/EUR for GBP/USD--EUR/USD; the full names appear on first use and in section and table headers, and the short forms in running prose. The four strategies are BH (passive Buy~\&~Hold), BL (the baseline cointegration $z$-score rule), AR (the binary AR-gated variant), and MS (the continuous MS-AR threshold variant). On the modelling side, HMM is a Hidden Markov Model, AR($p$) an order-$p$ autoregression, OLS ordinary least squares, ADF the Augmented Dickey--Fuller test, EM the expectation--maximisation algorithm, MR the mean-reverting (low-variance) regime, and DR the drifting (high-variance) regime. Reported quantities use SR for the Sharpe ratio, MDD for maximum drawdown, UI for the Ulcer index, VaR and CVaR for the $95\%$ Value-at-Risk and Conditional VaR, bps for basis points ($10^{-4}$), and RT for a round-trip trade. A full symbol dictionary appears at the end of the appendix in Section~\ref{sec:apx-symbols}.
