\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{The quantitative revolution}

Modern financial markets are mathematical objects. The trading pits, voice execution, and discretionary judgement of the past have been replaced by electronic limit-order books, automated market makers, statistical arbitrage engines, and latency-sensitive execution algorithms \parencite{lewis2014flashboys,aldridge2013hft,biais2015equilibrium}. Prices are no longer just economic summaries of fundamentals; they are also high-dimensional data streams generated by the interaction of agents, algorithms, inventories, order flows, and information shocks \parencite{ohara1995market,hasbrouck2007microstructure,hautsch2012econometrics}.

From a mathematical perspective, the deeper issue is not speed but \emph{non-stationarity}: financial time series are noisy, adaptive, and adversarial, so any exploitable structure is weak, transient, and rapidly arbitraged away \parencite{cont2001empirical}. The trader is no longer simply asking whether an asset is ``cheap'' or ``expensive'', but whether a small statistical regularity can be detected, validated, traded, and risk-managed after costs \parencite{lopezdeprado2018advances,aitsahalia2014}. Jim Simons is a useful symbol of this view: a mathematician who treated finance as a signal-processing problem rather than as economic forecasting \parencite{zuckerman2019man}.

\subsection{FX microstructure and pairs trading}

The foreign exchange (FX) market is one of the most natural settings in which to study this problem: it is globally traded, electronically intermediated, and active nearly twenty-four hours a day. At low frequencies, exchange rates are well approximated by continuous diffusions \parencite{bachelier1900theorie,blackscholes1973pricing,merton1973theory}. At the tick level, however, prices move in small discrete increments, quotes update asynchronously, and the price path becomes a sequence of small jumps and microstructural frictions \parencite{roll1984spread,jacod2017}. A single stationary Gaussian model does not capture this picture.

Currency pairs with shared economic or geographic exposure are natural candidates for \emph{pairs trading}: rather than betting on the level of one exchange rate, one bets on temporary deviations between two related ones reverting to a common stochastic trend \parencite{gatev2006pairs,vidyamurthy2004pairs,avellaneda2010statarb,krauss2017statarb}. The three constructions used here---AUD/USD vs NZD/USD, EUR/NOK vs EUR/SEK, and GBP/USD vs EUR/USD---span a range of liquidity and dependence regimes.

\subsection{Non-stationarity and regimes}

The main statistical difficulty is non-stationarity: volatility clusters, correlations rise and fall, and liquidity varies intraday \parencite{engle1982arch,bollerslev1986garch,cont2001empirical}. A trading rule that works in one regime may fail in another. This motivates the language of \emph{market regimes}: persistent latent states, each with its own statistical properties, switching according to some stochastic mechanism \parencite{hamilton1989new}.

We observe only the emitted data: returns, spreads, and trading outcomes. The natural modelling tool is therefore a Hidden Markov Model (HMM), which assumes that an observable process is driven by an unobserved Markov chain whose state determines the distribution of the observation \parencite{rabiner1989tutorial,cappe2005,krolzig1997msvar}.

\subsection{The role of the HMM in this thesis}\label{sec:roleofhmm}

The exercise is deliberately layered.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We build a single tradable signal: the rolling cointegration spread $S_t$ \parencite{engle1987cointegration,dickey1979distribution}, standardised into a $z$-score $Z_t$. The \emph{baseline} pairs-trading rule enters and exits positions on $Z_t$ alone \parencite{gatev2006pairs,vidyamurthy2004pairs}.
    \item We back-test the baseline first under realistic transaction costs. This establishes the benchmark against which any added complexity must justify itself.
    \item We then introduce a Markov-switching AR(1) on $S_t$ \parencite{hamilton1989new,krolzig1997msvar} to recover a hidden chain $K_t \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$ representing the active regime.
    \item The HMM is used \emph{as a regime gate on top of the baseline signal}, not as an independent forecaster. The trading direction stays dictated by $Z_t$; the HMM's role is to suppress or down-weight trading during high-volatility, weak-mean-reversion regimes. In short: \emph{the baseline gives the signal, the HMM decides when not to trade}.
\end{enumerate}

Because the regime-aware strategies inherit the baseline's direction, any difference between them and the baseline is attributable to the gate alone---which is why baseline results are reported \emph{before} regime-model results.
