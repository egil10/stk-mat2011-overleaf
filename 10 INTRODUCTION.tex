\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Modern financial markets are largely mathematical objects. The trading pits and human judgement of the past have been replaced by electronic order books, statistical arbitrage engines, and high-speed execution algorithms \parencite{aldridge2013hft}. Prices are no longer just summaries of economic fundamentals; they are dense data streams shaped by agents, algorithms, inventories, and news shocks \parencite{hasbrouck2007microstructure}. The hard part is not speed but non-stationarity: any pattern that does exist is small, short-lived, and quickly traded away \parencite{cont2001empirical}. The practical question becomes whether a small statistical regularity can be found, tested, traded, and risk-managed after costs \parencite{lopezdeprado2018advances}.

Foreign exchange (FX) is a natural setting for this problem. It is global, electronic, and open almost twenty-four hours a day. On daily scales, exchange rates look like continuous diffusions \parencite{blackscholes1973pricing}. At the tick level, prices move in small discrete jumps, quotes update asynchronously, and the path is dominated by microstructure frictions \parencite{roll1984spread}. Figure~\ref{fig:high-frequency-microstructure} shows this scale dependence directly: the daily view is roughly diffusive, the hourly view shows local mean reversion, and the tick view is mostly bid--ask bounce. Currency pairs with shared economic exposure are natural candidates for pairs trading, where one bets on short-term deviations between two related exchange rates reverting to a common trend \parencite{gatev2006pairs}. The three pairs we study (AUD/USD vs NZD/USD, EUR/NOK vs EUR/SEK, GBP/USD vs EUR/USD) span a range of liquidity and dependence.

A second difficulty is that each pair is itself non-stationary. Volatility clusters, correlations move around, and liquidity varies through the day \parencite{cont2001empirical}, so a rule that works in one period may fail in another. This is the language of market regimes: persistent hidden states, each with its own statistical behaviour, switching between one another by some random mechanism \parencite{hamilton1989new}. Because the regime is unobservable and we only see the data it produces, the natural tool is a Hidden Markov Model (HMM), where an observed series is driven by an unobserved Markov chain whose state determines the distribution of each observation \parencite{rabiner1989tutorial}.

\paragraph{Role of the HMM in this thesis.}\label{sec:roleofhmm}
The set-up is layered. We first build a single tradable signal: the rolling cointegration spread $S_t$ \parencite{engle1987cointegration}, standardised into a $z$-score $Z_t$, plugged into a textbook pairs-trading rule \parencite{gatev2006pairs}. This baseline is back-tested under realistic transaction costs and serves as the benchmark that any more complex method has to beat. We then fit a Markov-switching AR(1) to the same $S_t$ \parencite{hamilton1989new} to recover a hidden chain $K_t$ representing the active regime, and use it as a gate on top of the baseline rather than as a separate forecaster. The trading direction is always set by $Z_t$, and the entry rule is identical to the baseline; the HMM only decides whether the rule is allowed to act at a given bar. The regime-aware strategies cannot enter a trade the baseline does not enter, nor flip a position the baseline does not flip; they can only suppress trading in high-volatility regimes. Any difference in performance between the baseline and the regime-aware variants is therefore due to the gate alone, which is why baseline results are reported first. Figure~\ref{fig:true-and-hidden-states} previews this regime recovery on a simulated two-regime AR(1).
