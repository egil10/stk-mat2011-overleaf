\section{Regime-aware models}\label{sec:regimemodels}

We introduce the AR/HMM machinery on a generic series $Y_t$ and then apply it to the spread $S_t$. An autoregressive model of order $p$ predicts $Y_t$ from its $p$ most recent past values \parencite{hamilton1994},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arp}
    Y_t \;=\; c \;+\; \phi_{1} Y_{t-1} \;+\; \cdots \;+\; \phi_{p} Y_{t-p} \;+\; \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),
\end{equation}
with the simplest non-trivial case AR(1),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ar1}
    Y_t \;=\; c + \phi\, Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t,
\end{equation}
which has unconditional mean $c/(1-\phi)$ when $|\phi|<1$. AR(1) describes either momentum ($\phi > 0$) or fast mean reversion ($\phi < 0$), but only if the parameters $(c,\phi,\sigma^{2})$ stay constant throughout the sample. We relax that constancy assumption by letting the parameters switch between a small set of values according to an unobserved Markov chain $K_t \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$ \parencite{hamilton1989new}. The chain is described by a $K \times K$ transition matrix $\mathbf{P} = (p_{ij})$ and an initial distribution $\nu = (\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_K)^{\top}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transitionmatrix}
    p_{ij} \;=\; \mathbb{P}(K_t = j \mid K_{t-1} = i),
    \qquad
    \nu_k \;=\; \mathbb{P}(K_1 = k),
\end{equation}
with row sums $\sum_{j=1}^{K} p_{ij} = 1$ for every $i$. The Markov assumption is deliberately simple: the future depends on the past only through the current state. But the model is still rich enough to capture persistence, sudden switching, and regime-dependent parameters in a single probabilistic story.

\subsection{Markov-switching AR(1) on the spread}\label{sec:msarspread}

We now specialise to $S_t$ from \eqref{eq:rollingspreadret} and take $K = 2$. Conditional on the active regime $K_t = k$, the spread follows an AR(1) with its own intercept, AR coefficient, and innovation variance:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:msargen}
\begin{aligned}
    S_t \mid S_{t-1},\, K_t = k \;&\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(c^{(k)} + \rho^{(k)} S_{t-1},\;(\sigma^{(k)})^{2}\bigr),\\[4pt]
    \mathbf{P} \;&=\; \begin{pmatrix} p_{11} & p_{12} \\ p_{21} & p_{22} \end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
All three regime-level parameters $(c^{(k)},\rho^{(k)},\sigma^{(k)})$ switch with the chain \parencite{krolzig1997msvar}. For $|\rho^{(k)}|<1$ the regime-$k$ unconditional mean is $\mu^{(k)} = c^{(k)}/(1-\rho^{(k)})$, which lets us write the same model in mean-deviation form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:meandevparam}
    S_t \;=\; \mu^{(k)} \;+\; \rho^{(k)}\bigl(S_{t-1} - \mu^{(k)}\bigr) \;+\; \varepsilon_t^{(k)},
    \qquad
    \varepsilon_t^{(k)} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\,(\sigma^{(k)})^{2}\bigr).
\end{equation}
A $\rho^{(k)}$ near zero means the spread reverts quickly; a $\rho^{(k)}$ near one means it behaves almost like a random walk. Before estimation, $S_t$ is winsorised at $\mathrm{median}(S) \pm 4\cdot\mathrm{std}(S)$ and rescaled by $10^{4}$ for numerical stability.

\paragraph{Estimation, regime choice, and labelling.} The HMM parameter set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thetadef}
    \Theta \;=\; \bigl\{\, \nu,\; \mathbf{P},\; c^{(k)},\; \rho^{(k)},\; \sigma^{(k)} : k=1,\ldots,K \,\bigr\}
\end{equation}
is fitted by maximum likelihood using the Markov-switching routine in the \texttt{statsmodels} package \parencite{seabold2010statsmodels}, restarted from several random seeds to reduce sensitivity to local maxima. The same fit produces the smoothed regime posteriors
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smoothedposterior}
    \gamma_t(k) \;=\; \mathbb{P}(K_t = k \mid S_1,\ldots,S_T;\Theta).
\end{equation}
We fix $K = 2$ throughout. A two-state model (one ``normal'' low-volatility regime and one ``stress'' high-volatility regime) is the standard parsimonious choice in the regime-switching literature \parencite{hamilton1989new}; in our preliminary exploration $K \in \{3, 4\}$ either failed to separate cleanly across seeds or split the high-volatility state into sub-regimes whose entry implications were indistinguishable at our cost level. With $K=2$ we label the two regimes by innovation variance,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:regimelabels}
    k_{\mathrm{MR}} \;=\; \arg\min_{k}\,\sigma^{(k)},
    \qquad
    k_{\mathrm{DR}} \;=\; \arg\max_{k}\,\sigma^{(k)},
\end{equation}
so the low-variance state is called the mean-reverting regime (MR) and the high-variance state the drifting regime (DR). We write $\gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}} = \mathbb{P}(K_t = k_{\mathrm{MR}} \mid S_1,\ldots,S_T)$ and $\gamma_t^{\mathrm{DR}} = 1 - \gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}}$. In live trading only past data is available, so the back-test uses the filtered posterior $\mathbb{P}(K_t = k_{\mathrm{MR}} \mid S_1,\ldots,S_t)$ inside the gate; the smoothed posterior is kept for the diagnostic figures.

\subsection{Regime-aware strategies}\label{sec:regimestrategies}

We build two regime-aware strategies on top of the baseline \eqref{eq:baselinerule}. Both keep the baseline's entry direction and exit logic untouched: at every bar the candidate position is $\pi_t^{\mathrm{baseline}}$ from \eqref{eq:baselinerule}, and the regime model can only veto or attenuate that candidate. Neither variant can generate a position the baseline did not generate, nor reverse a position the baseline took.

\paragraph{AR-gated.} A binary gate with danger threshold $\delta \in (0,1)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:argate}
    G_t \;=\; \mathbbm{1}\!\bigl\{\,\gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}} \,\geq\, 1-\delta\,\bigr\},
\end{equation}
modifies the position rule to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arpositionrule}
    \pi_t \;=\;
    \begin{cases}
        0 & \text{if } G_t = 0 \quad\text{(liquidate, stay flat)},\\[2pt]
        \pi_t^{\mathrm{baseline}} & \text{if } G_t = 1.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The gate is binary: either trade exactly like the baseline, or sit in cash. It also closes existing positions: if we are long the spread and the HMM flags danger, we go flat at the next bar.

\paragraph{MS-AR.} A continuous, regime-weighted entry threshold,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:msarthreshold}
    z_q^{\mathrm{eff}}(t) \;=\; \gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}}\, z_q \;+\; \gamma_t^{\mathrm{DR}}\, z_v,
    \qquad\text{with } z_v \geq z_q,
\end{equation}
replaces $z_q$ in \eqref{eq:baselinerule} by $z_q^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)$. When the MR regime is highly probable, $z_q^{\mathrm{eff}} \approx z_q$ and the strategy trades like the baseline. When the DR regime dominates, the threshold rises towards $z_v$ and the rule needs a bigger $|Z_t|$ deviation before it enters.

Table~\ref{tab:strategy_definitions} summarises the four strategies compared throughout the rest of the thesis. The three active rules all share the same spread $S_t$ and $z$-score $Z_t$, and differ only in whether and how $\gamma_t^{\mathrm{MR}}$ enters the decision. AR and MS-AR are not separate forecasters: on any bar where the HMM does not intervene they trade exactly like the baseline, so any difference between baseline and the regime-aware variants comes entirely from the gate suppressing or attenuating baseline trades.
